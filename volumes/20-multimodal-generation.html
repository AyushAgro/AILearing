Generative Modelling Landscape

Every generator is a way to draw samples from a distribution you cannot write down. The families differ in what they learn, how many network evaluations one sample costs, whether they give you a likelihood, and which failure you will be debugging at 2 a.m. This chapter trains one small member of each family on the same data and compares them with the same probes.

The Engineering Problem

A product needs images from a prompt, speech from text, and a few seconds of video. Likelihood models, adversarial models, autoregressive models, diffusion and flows all claim to be the answer. They do not have the same training stability, the same serving cost or the same failure catalogue. Teams that choose from a demo reel end up with a sampler they cannot budget and a failure mode they did not plan to monitor.

Possible Solutions

  • Variational autoencoder (VAE). Learn an encoder and decoder with a Gaussian latent. One decoder pass per sample. Does not solve sharpness: a Gaussian decoder averages what the latent cannot separate.
  • Generative adversarial network (GAN). A generator fools a discriminator. One pass, sharp samples. Does not solve stability or coverage: training is a two-player game and modes get dropped silently.
  • Autoregressive (AR) model. Factor the joint into a product of conditionals, one token at a time. Exact likelihood and a stable loss. Does not solve latency: one sequential pass per token, and pixels or audio samples are many tokens.
  • Diffusion. Learn to undo a fixed Gaussian noising process. Stable regression loss, excellent coverage. Does not solve latency either: tens to a thousand network calls per sample.
  • Flow matching. Learn a velocity field that transports noise to data along a chosen path, then integrate an ODE. The same stability as diffusion, with paths that can be made straight enough for a few steps. Does not remove the solver: the step count is still a measured hyperparameter.

Five Objectives in One Page

Each family optimises a different quantity. Writing them side by side makes their failure modes predictable before any code runs.

\text{VAE:}\quad \mathcal{L} = \mathbb{E}_{q_\phi(z\mid x)}\big[-\log p_\theta(x\mid z)\big] + \mathrm{KL}\big(q_\phi(z\mid x)\,\|\,\mathcal{N}(0, I)\big)
\text{GAN:}\quad \min_G \max_D\; \mathbb{E}_{x\sim p_{\text{data}}}[\log D(x)] + \mathbb{E}_{z}[\log(1 - D(G(z)))]
\text{AR:}\quad \log p(x) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \log p(x_i \mid x_{<i})
\text{Diffusion:}\quad \mathcal{L} = \mathbb{E}_{x_0, \epsilon, t}\,\big\|\epsilon - \epsilon_\theta(\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,x_0 + \sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,\epsilon,\ t)\big\|^2
\text{Flow matching:}\quad \mathcal{L} = \mathbb{E}_{x_0, x_1, t}\,\big\|(x_1 - x_0) - v_\theta\big((1-t)\,x_0 + t\,x_1,\ t\big)\big\|^2
  • The VAE's first term is a mean squared error under a Gaussian decoder. MSE is minimised by the conditional mean, and the mean of two modes lies between them. That is the blur.
  • The GAN's objective never rewards covering every mode. A generator that produces six convincing modes out of eight can fool a discriminator locally. That is mode dropping.
  • The AR objective is exact, which is why it is the only family here that reports a held-out likelihood without a bound. The price is n sequential calls.
  • Diffusion and flow matching are both plain regressions on a synthetic target. There is no adversary and no bound to tighten: stable training is the main reason they won images, audio and video.

A Shared Testbed

All five families run on the same data: eight Gaussian modes of standard deviation 0.15 on a ring of radius 2. The data is simple enough to train in seconds and structured enough to expose each failure. Three probes read a batch of 1,000 samples: precision (the share within three standard deviations of a mode), modes covered (modes receiving at least 2% of samples), and the KL divergence of the mode histogram from uniform. A kernel maximum mean discrepancy (MMD) against 1,000 held-out real points summarises everything in one number.

The denoisers and generators in this volume are all the same from-scratch MLP: SiLU hidden layers, a hand-written backward pass, Adam, global-norm gradient clipping, and an exponential moving average (EMA) of the weights for sampling. It is the Tier L stand-in for a U-Net or a diffusion transformer: same inputs (noisy sample, time, condition), same output, far fewer parameters.

An Autoregressive Model with a Table for a Network

With two coordinates, an autoregressive model is two tokens. Discretise each coordinate into 64 bins; then p(x_1)\,p(x_2 \mid x_1) can be estimated exactly by counting. This is a simplified AR model: the "network" is a lookup table, which only works because the sequence has length 2. At 64 pixels the table would need 64^{64} cells, which is why real AR image and audio models are Transformers over learned discrete codes (VQ-VAE, VQGAN, EnCodec).

Why Diffusion and Flows Won, and What Came Back

  • Scalability of the loss. A regression target with no adversary lets you scale model and data the way language models scale. Dhariwal and Nichol (2021) showed diffusion beating the best GANs on ImageNet FID; nearly every major image system since has been diffusion or flow based. Documented for DALL·E 2 (Ramesh et al., 2022), Imagen (Saharia et al., 2022), Stable Diffusion (Rombach et al., 2022) and Stable Diffusion 3 (Esser et al., 2024).
  • Coverage. Diffusion's objective penalises every region of the data distribution; no adversary gets to ignore a mode.
  • Latents made it affordable. Running diffusion in an autoencoder's latent (Chapter 5) cut compute enough to serve.
  • The other families did not disappear. VAEs are the first stage of latent diffusion, GAN losses train the autoencoders and vocoders, and autoregressive Transformers over codec tokens dominate speech and music generation (Chapter 8). Inferred Closed products such as Midjourney have not published their architectures; the common claim that they are diffusion models is an inference from industry practice, not a documented fact.
Key Idea
  • Choose a family by the serving constraint (one pass versus fifty), by whether you need a likelihood or discrete tokens, and by the failure you can tolerate: blur, mode loss or slowness.
  • Diffusion and flow matching turn generation into supervised regression on synthetic targets. That is why they train reliably, and why their cost moved from training instability to sampling steps.
  • Choosing a family from a screenshot. Precision and coverage are separate axes; the GAN above has decent precision and a skewed mode histogram.
  • Reading a VAE's blur as under-training. It is the Gaussian likelihood averaging modes. More steps do not fix it; a sharper decoder likelihood, a perceptual or adversarial loss, or a second-stage prior does.
  • Extrapolating a 2-D win. The AR table wins here only because the sequence has two tokens.

Diffusion from First Principles

Diffusion defines a forward process that destroys data with Gaussian noise in many small steps, then trains a network to predict the noise that was added. Sampling starts from pure noise and walks backwards. This chapter derives the pieces you need to implement it, checks the forward process against its closed form, trains a DDPM from scratch, and reads the loss curve by timestep.

The Engineering Problem

You can draw samples from a standard Gaussian. You cannot draw samples from "photographs" or even from "eight blobs on a ring" without a model. You want a training loss that is mean squared error, because you know how to optimise it at scale; a model with no adversary; and a procedure that turns Gaussian noise into data with a knob for quality versus compute.

Possible Solutions

  • Score matching with a single noise level. Learn \nabla_x \log p(x) directly. Fails where data is sparse: the score is estimated badly away from the data, exactly where sampling starts.
  • Annealed score matching over many noise levels. Estimate scores of progressively noised data and anneal a Langevin sampler. Works, but the sampler and noise levels are hand-tuned.
  • Diffusion probabilistic models. Fix a Markov chain that adds noise; learn its reversal with a variational bound (Sohl-Dickstein et al., 2015). DDPM (Ho et al., 2020) showed a simplified noise-prediction loss makes this competitive, and Song et al. (2021) unified it with score matching as a stochastic differential equation.

The Forward Process

Choose a variance schedule \beta_1, \dots, \beta_T and define a Markov chain that shrinks the signal and adds noise:

q(x_t \mid x_{t-1}) = \mathcal{N}\big(x_t;\ \sqrt{1-\beta_t}\,x_{t-1},\ \beta_t I\big)

With \alpha_t = 1 - \beta_t and \bar\alpha_t = \prod_{s\le t}\alpha_s, Gaussians compose, and any step can be sampled in one shot from the clean data:

q(x_t \mid x_0) = \mathcal{N}\big(x_t;\ \sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,x_0,\ (1-\bar\alpha_t) I\big) \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad x_t = \sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,x_0 + \sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,\epsilon,\ \ \epsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0, I)
  • This closed form is what makes training cheap: pick a random t per example, jump there, and never simulate the chain.
  • The useful summary of a noise level is the signal-to-noise ratio \mathrm{SNR}(t) = \bar\alpha_t / (1-\bar\alpha_t). Schedules differ in how they spend steps across log-SNR.
  • The last step must be (nearly) pure noise. If \bar\alpha_T is not close to 0, sampling from \mathcal{N}(0, I) starts outside the distribution the network was trained on.

The Reverse Process and the Noise-Prediction Loss

The reverse of each small Gaussian step is approximately Gaussian. Conditioned on x_0, it is exactly Gaussian with a known mean:

q(x_{t-1}\mid x_t, x_0) = \mathcal{N}\big(\tilde\mu_t(x_t, x_0),\ \tilde\beta_t I\big),\quad \tilde\mu_t = \frac{\sqrt{\bar\alpha_{t-1}}\,\beta_t}{1-\bar\alpha_t}x_0 + \frac{\sqrt{\alpha_t}\,(1-\bar\alpha_{t-1})}{1-\bar\alpha_t}x_t,\quad \tilde\beta_t = \frac{1-\bar\alpha_{t-1}}{1-\bar\alpha_t}\beta_t

We do not know x_0 at sampling time, but we can estimate it from x_t. Substituting x_0 = (x_t - \sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,\epsilon)/\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t} turns the mean into a function of the noise:

\mu_\theta(x_t, t) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_t}}\Big(x_t - \frac{\beta_t}{\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}}\,\epsilon_\theta(x_t, t)\Big)

Ho et al. (2020) showed that the variational bound reduces, up to per-timestep weights, to a regression of the noise, and that dropping those weights (the "simple" loss) gives better samples:

\mathcal{L}_{\text{simple}} = \mathbb{E}_{t\sim\mathcal{U}\{1..T\},\ x_0,\ \epsilon}\ \big\|\epsilon - \epsilon_\theta\big(\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,x_0 + \sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,\epsilon,\ t\big)\big\|^2
Three Parameterisations, One Model
  • ε-prediction (DDPM). Predict the noise. Well-scaled at every t because the target always has unit variance.
  • x0-prediction. Predict the clean sample. Equivalent by the identity above, but errors at high noise are amplified when converted back to ε.
  • v-prediction (Salimans and Ho, 2022). Predict v = \sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,\epsilon - \sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,x_0. Well-behaved at both ends and common in production latent models.
  • The score is the same quantity rescaled: \nabla_{x_t}\log q(x_t) \approx -\epsilon_\theta(x_t, t)/\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}. That identity is the bridge to the score-SDE view below and to flow matching in Chapter 4.

Ancestral Sampling

Sampling is the reverse chain: start at x_T\sim\mathcal{N}(0, I), and for each t compute \mu_\theta and add fresh noise with variance \sigma_t^2. Ho et al. found \sigma_t^2 = \beta_t and \sigma_t^2 = \tilde\beta_t gave similar results; the listing uses \beta_t. At t = 0 no noise is added.

The Chapter 1 table already scored this sampler: precision 0.948, all eight modes, KL 0.003, MMD 0.0045, which equals the exact AR model's MMD on this data. It took 1,000 network calls per sample. The next chapter is about that number.

Implementation Notes
  • Feed the network t/T (or log-SNR), not the integer t. A continuous time input lets you change T or skip steps without retraining the embedding.
  • Sample from the EMA weights. On this run the raw weights at step 4,000 are noisier than their average; production diffusion training typically uses EMA decay 0.999 to 0.9999 over far longer runs.
  • Initialise the last layer small (the MLP scales it by 0.1). A denoiser that starts by predicting ε ≈ 0 has a sane first-step loss of about 1.
  • Test the forward process, then overfit a single batch, then train. Diffusion bugs rarely crash; they produce plausible-looking noise.

The Continuous-Time View

Song et al. (2021) took T \to \infty. The forward chain becomes a stochastic differential equation, and its reversal needs only the score \nabla_x \log p_t(x), which the ε-network provides:

dx = f(x, t)\,dt + g(t)\,dw \qquad\longrightarrow\qquad dx = \big[f(x,t) - g(t)^2\,\nabla_x\log p_t(x)\big]dt + g(t)\,d\bar w

The same paper shows a deterministic probability-flow ODE with the same marginals:

\frac{dx}{dt} = f(x,t) - \tfrac{1}{2}\,g(t)^2\,\nabla_x \log p_t(x)
  • Once sampling is "solve an ODE", any ODE solver applies. DDIM (Chapter 3) is a first-order solver of this ODE in disguise; DPM-Solver, Heun and the Diffusers schedulers are higher-order ones.
  • A deterministic map from noise to data also gives exact likelihoods and encoding (run the ODE backwards), which Chapter 7's editing methods rely on.
  • Flow matching (Chapter 4) starts from the ODE directly and skips the SDE.

Sampling

A trained denoiser defines a distribution; the sampler decides how many network calls you pay to reach it. DDPM takes one call per training step and injects fresh noise. DDIM follows a deterministic path and can skip steps. Distillation trains a student to jump most of the way at once. The quality-versus-steps curve is a product decision, and you have to measure it on your own model.

The Engineering Problem

The Chapter 2 model needed 1,000 network calls per sample. On an image model each call is a full U-Net or Transformer forward over a latent grid, so 1,000 calls is minutes of GPU time per image. Users will not wait, and nobody will pay for it. You need a sampler that visits a subset of noise levels without retraining, a way to measure what the subset costs in quality, and, when that is still too slow, a way to train the steps away.

Possible Solutions

  • Stride the DDPM chain. Use every tenth step with the ancestral update. Cheap to try; the Markov update was derived for adjacent steps, so quality falls quickly.
  • DDIM (Song, Meng and Ermon, 2020). A non-Markovian process with the same marginals, whose update can jump between any two noise levels, deterministically if you want. No retraining.
  • Higher-order ODE solvers (Heun, DPM-Solver, UniPC). Treat sampling as the probability-flow ODE and use a better integrator. No retraining; more calls per step.
  • Better schedules and learned variances (Nichol and Dhariwal, 2021). Make fewer steps less damaging.
  • Distillation and consistency models. Train a student that reaches the answer in one to four calls. Needs a second training run and usually a quality drop, but it is the only route to single-digit call counts.

DDIM: Predict x0, Then Re-noise

DDIM rewrites each step as two moves. First, use the noise estimate to predict the clean sample. Then place that prediction at the previous noise level, reusing the same noise direction:

\hat x_0 = \frac{x_t - \sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,\epsilon_\theta(x_t, t)}{\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}}, \qquad x_{s} = \sqrt{\bar\alpha_{s}}\,\hat x_0 + \sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_{s} - \sigma_t^2}\;\epsilon_\theta(x_t, t) + \sigma_t\, z
\sigma_t = \eta\,\sqrt{\frac{1-\bar\alpha_{s}}{1-\bar\alpha_t}}\sqrt{1 - \frac{\bar\alpha_t}{\bar\alpha_{s}}}
  • s is any earlier timestep, not necessarily t-1. That is the whole trick: the update never assumed adjacent steps.
  • \eta = 0 gives a deterministic map from x_T to x_0: a first-order solver of the probability-flow ODE. \eta = 1 matches the DDPM posterior variance.
  • The same trained ε-network serves every sampler. Samplers are inference-time choices, so they belong in configuration, not in the checkpoint.

Distillation: Train the Steps Away

A deterministic sampler is a function from noise to data. Nothing stops you from learning that function directly. The simplest version (knowledge distillation of the sampler) runs the teacher once per training pair and regresses a student onto it. Progressive distillation halves the steps repeatedly; consistency models learn a function that maps any point on a trajectory to its end; adversarial and distribution-matching distillation add losses so the one-step student is sharp rather than an average.

Flow Matching and Rectified Flow

Flow matching trains a velocity field that carries noise to data along a path you choose, then samples by integrating an ODE. The straight-line path is the rectified-flow special case; reflowing its own samples makes the paths straighter still. Stable Diffusion 3, FLUX.1 and Meta's Movie Gen all document training with this objective. This chapter derives it, implements it, compares solvers, and makes a one-step sampler by reflow.

The Engineering Problem

Diffusion's probability-flow ODE curves: the trajectory from a noise sample to its data point bends, so a coarse solver cuts corners and lands between modes (the DDIM one-step failure). The noise schedule, the parameterisation and the loss weighting are three coupled knobs. You would rather choose a simple path, regress its velocity with plain MSE, and integrate with a handful of steps, with the path itself as the only design choice.

Possible Solutions

  • Continuous normalising flows trained by maximum likelihood. Exact, but every training step integrates an ODE and a divergence. Too slow to scale.
  • Diffusion with a better solver. Keeps the curved paths; improves the integrator.
  • Flow matching (Lipman et al., 2022). Regress a velocity field onto per-sample conditional velocities along any chosen path. Simulation-free like diffusion.
  • Rectified flow (Liu, Gong and Liu, 2022). The straight-line path, plus a "reflow" procedure that re-pairs noise and data using the model's own ODE so that paths straighten.

Derivation in Four Lines

Take noise x_0\sim\mathcal{N}(0, I), data x_1, and the straight interpolant with its constant velocity:

x_t = (1-t)\,x_0 + t\,x_1,\qquad \frac{dx_t}{dt} = x_1 - x_0,\qquad t\in[0,1]

The marginal velocity field that moves the whole distribution is the conditional expectation of that per-pair velocity given where you are:

u_t(x) = \mathbb{E}\big[x_1 - x_0 \,\big|\, x_t = x\big]

We cannot compute u_t, but we can regress onto its per-sample target, because the minimiser of a squared error is the conditional expectation. This is the conditional flow-matching loss, and its gradient equals that of the intractable marginal loss:

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{CFM}}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{t\sim\mathcal{U}[0,1],\ x_0,\ x_1}\ \big\|v_\theta(x_t, t) - (x_1 - x_0)\big\|^2

Sampling integrates dx/dt = v_\theta(x, t) from t = 0 to t = 1. With the Gaussian path this is closely related to diffusion: the velocity is an affine function of the ε-prediction, v = (x_t - \epsilon)/t at the optimum in this convention, so the two models carry the same information in different units.

Key Idea
  • Flow matching is diffusion with the path chosen directly: no β schedule, no SDE, one line of arithmetic for the training pair.
  • The per-pair paths are straight; the marginal ODE paths are not, because many pairs cross at the same x_t and the network learns their average direction. Reflow removes the crossings.
  • Convention warning: this volume and Lipman et al. use t = 0 for noise. SD3 and Diffusers' FlowMatchEulerDiscreteScheduler use t = 1 (σ = 1) for noise. The algebra is identical with t \to 1 - t and a sign flip on the velocity.

Latent Diffusion

Pixels are a wasteful space to denoise. Most of their bits are predictable texture that the eye barely notices. Latent diffusion trains an autoencoder once, runs diffusion or flow in its small latent, and decodes once at the end. This chapter builds the whole stack on 8×8 digits: a probe classifier for evaluation, an autoencoder sweep, a latent scale factor, and a head-to-head of latent versus pixel generation. It ends with the backbone question: U-Net or diffusion transformer.

The Engineering Problem

A 1024×1024 RGB image has about three million numbers. A denoiser that processes all of them at every one of 30 steps, twice for guidance, does not meet an interactive latency budget. Most of those numbers are redundant. You want a much smaller representation that still reconstructs what people notice, and you want the expensive iterative model to live there, while the decoder runs once.

Possible Solutions

  • Pixel-space cascades (Imagen, DALL·E 2's decoder). Generate at 64×64, then run diffusion super-resolution models. Keeps pixel fidelity; pays for several diffusion models.
  • Discrete latents with an autoregressive or masked prior (VQ-VAE, VQGAN, MaskGIT). Compress to tokens and model them like language. Good compression; quantisation artefacts and token-by-token cost.
  • Continuous latent diffusion (Rombach et al., 2022). A KL- or VQ-regularised autoencoder with a perceptual and adversarial loss, then diffusion in its latent. The design behind Stable Diffusion and most open image and video models since.

An Evaluation Probe First

Before building a generator, build the instrument that will judge it. For 8×8 digits a small classifier plays two roles that production systems split between Inception (for FID) and CLIP (for prompt adherence): its accuracy on generated digits measures whether the sample matches the requested class, and its hidden layer is the feature space for a Fréchet distance. Chapter 10 treats this properly; here it is a gate.

A generated set scoring 5 or 6 is therefore close to "as good as another real sample", and nobody should read a toy FD as an absolute quality.

The Autoencoder and Its Scale Factor

The autoencoder here is an MLP: 64 pixels to a d-dimensional code and back, with a tiny L2 penalty on the code standing in for the KL term. After training, latents are rescaled to unit variance. Stable Diffusion does exactly this with a constant stored in the VAE config; Documented the SD 1.x VAE's scaling_factor is 0.18215 in its Diffusers configuration. Forget the factor and the denoiser trains on latents whose variance does not match the \mathcal{N}(0, I) prior the sampler starts from.

  • The probe's accuracy on reconstructions saturates at width 8 (0.980, equal to its accuracy on real digits). Width 16 reconstructs pixels better but no more recognisably. The semantic ceiling arrives before the pixel ceiling.
  • Width 2 loses a tenth of the digits' identity. A perfect denoiser in that latent still generates the wrong digit 10% of the time: the autoencoder is a ceiling the generator cannot exceed.
  • The scale factor drifts with width (0.92 to 1.39). It is a property of each trained autoencoder, which is why it lives in the autoencoder's config, not the denoiser's.

Rombach et al. found the same trade-off at image scale: very small downsampling factors leave the denoiser too much work, very large ones destroy detail, and factors of 4 to 16 were the sweet spot in their comparisons, with f = 8 becoming the Stable Diffusion default.

Conditioning and Guidance

An unconditional generator draws from the data distribution. A product draws from the distribution given a prompt, a class, an edge map or a pose. Conditioning gets the information into the denoiser; guidance decides how hard the sampler leans on it. This chapter implements cross-attention, trains a class-conditional latent flow with classifier-free guidance, measures the adherence–diversity trade-off, and shows why ControlNet's zero-initialised layers let you add a new control without breaking the model.

The Engineering Problem

A conditional model trained with the condition as an input still produces samples that only loosely follow it, especially for unusual prompts. Users want a single knob that trades diversity for prompt adherence. Designers want spatial control (edges, depth, pose) on top of a model that cost millions to train, without retraining it. Both must fit a serving budget that already pays for tens of denoiser calls per image.

Possible Solutions

  • Concatenate or add the condition (class one-hot, pooled text embedding). Simple; one vector cannot describe "a red cube on a blue sphere".
  • Cross-attention to token sequences. Every spatial position queries the prompt's tokens. The latent-diffusion interface.
  • Classifier guidance (Dhariwal and Nichol, 2021). Add the gradient of a noisy-image classifier. Works; needs a separate classifier trained at every noise level.
  • Classifier-free guidance (Ho and Salimans, 2022). Train one model with the condition sometimes dropped; extrapolate between conditional and unconditional predictions at sampling time.
  • Adapters for new conditions (ControlNet, T2I-Adapter, IP-Adapter). Add a trainable branch to a frozen model.

Classifier Guidance

Bayes' rule splits the conditional score into the unconditional score plus a classifier term:

\nabla_{x_t}\log p(x_t\mid y) = \nabla_{x_t}\log p(x_t) + \nabla_{x_t}\log p(y\mid x_t)

Dhariwal and Nichol scaled the second term by a factor s, using a classifier trained on noisy images. Scaling above 1 sharpens the conditional distribution toward samples the classifier is confident about: higher precision and lower diversity. The cost is a second network that must be trained at every noise level and differentiated at every step.

Classifier-Free Guidance

Ho and Salimans removed the classifier. Train one network on both tasks by replacing the condition with a null token with probability p_{\text{uncond}} (0.1 in this volume). Since \nabla\log p(y\mid x_t) = \nabla\log p(x_t\mid y) - \nabla\log p(x_t), the classifier's gradient is the difference of the two predictions the model already makes:

\tilde\epsilon_\theta(x_t, y) = \epsilon_\theta(x_t, \varnothing) + s\,\big(\epsilon_\theta(x_t, y) - \epsilon_\theta(x_t, \varnothing)\big)

The identical formula applies to velocities in flow matching. s = 0 is unconditional, s = 1 is the plain conditional model, and s > 1 extrapolates past it. The original paper writes (1 + w)\epsilon_c - w\,\epsilon_u, so its w equals s - 1; Diffusers' guidance_scale is s. Mixing the two conventions is a real bug: w = 7.5 is s = 8.5.

The training change is the two lines in train_ddpm and train_fm that overwrite a random 10% of labels with the null index. The sampling change is velocity (or predict_eps), which concatenates the conditional and null inputs into one batch, runs one forward, and splits the result. ControlNet: Adding a Spatial Condition Without Breaking the Model

A new condition such as a Canny edge map or a pose skeleton needs new parameters. Fine-tuning the whole model on a small paired dataset destroys what it knew. ControlNet makes a trainable copy of the encoder blocks, feeds the condition into the copy, and connects the copy back through zero-initialised 1×1 convolutions. At initialisation the added branch contributes exactly zero, so the model's output is unchanged, yet the gradient reaching the zero layers is not zero, so training can start.

Image Editing

Editing is sampling with a constraint from an image you already have. How far you push the source into noise sets the edit strength; which pixels you pin sets the region; which attention maps you keep sets the layout. This chapter measures SDEdit's fidelity–freedom curve, implements inpainting that keeps known pixels exactly, and shows why prompt-to-prompt attention injection changes only what the swapped word controls.

The Engineering Problem

A user uploads a photo and asks for the same composition with a different object, a filled-in region, or a new style. Generating from pure noise ignores the upload; copying the upload ignores the instruction. You need a continuous, predictable trade between fidelity to the source and freedom to follow the edit, a guarantee that unedited regions stay untouched, and a mapping from a UI slider to the sampler that behaves the same on every model you deploy.

Possible Solutions

  • SDEdit (Meng et al., 2021). Noise the source to an intermediate level and denoise under the new condition. No training; one knob.
  • Inpainting. Generate only inside a mask, either by re-imposing known pixels at every step (training-free) or with a model trained to take the mask as input.
  • Attention-based editing (Prompt-to-Prompt, Hertz et al., 2022). Keep the source's cross-attention maps while changing the prompt, so layout survives.
  • Inversion (DDIM inversion, null-text inversion). Find the noise that reproduces a real image, then edit from there.
  • Personalisation (textual inversion, DreamBooth, LoRA). Teach the model a new subject from a few images, then generate it in new contexts.

SDEdit: Strength Is a Noise Level

In the flow convention the straight path makes SDEdit one line: move the source back to time t_0 = 1 - \text{strength} by mixing in noise, then integrate forward to t = 1 under the target condition.

z_{t_0} = (1 - t_0)\,\epsilon + t_0\, z_{\text{src}},\qquad z_{\text{edit}} = \mathrm{ODE}_{t_0 \to 1}\big(z_{t_0};\ v_\theta(\cdot, \cdot, y_{\text{target}})\big)

The experiment turns forty real 3s into 8s. Two numbers are read at each strength: whether the probe now sees an 8 (freedom), and pixel distance to the source (fidelity).

Prompt-to-Prompt: Keep the Maps, Change the Words

Regenerating with a new prompt, even from the same noise, changes every attention map and therefore the layout. Hertz et al. observed that layout lives in the cross-attention maps, so they inject the source prompt's maps while using the new prompt's values. The runnable version on the Chapter 6 cross-attention layer:

new prompt, fresh maps: max |attn_b - attn_a| = 0.177
P2P: output change per patch is proportional to attention on the swapped token:
  corr(change, attn[:, 1]) = 1.0
  least-attending patch: attn=0.08 change=0.249; most-attending: attn=0.28 change=0.866

With injected maps, each patch's output changes by exactly its attention weight on the swapped token times the change in that token's value (correlation 1.0). Patches that barely attend to "cat" barely change. That is the mechanism by which the background survives a word swap. In a real model it holds per layer and step; the paper injects maps only for an early fraction of steps, so the new object can take its own shape.

Audio Generation

Waveforms are long: 16,000 to 48,000 numbers per second. Generation became practical when audio was turned into a short sequence of discrete codes by a neural codec, so that a language model or a diffusion model could generate the codes and a decoder could turn them back into sound. This chapter builds the pieces that are runnable at Tier L (μ-law, a residual vector quantiser, the token-rate arithmetic, the MusicGen delay pattern, Griffin-Lim phase recovery, a WER round trip) and places the production codecs, codec language models, TTS systems and vocoders around them.

The Engineering Problem

A text-to-speech feature must speak a sentence in under a second, sound natural, pronounce what was written, and, if it imitates a voice, do so only with that person's consent. An autoregressive model over raw samples makes 16,000 sequential predictions per second of audio. A model over spectrograms must still recover phase. A model over codec tokens depends on how good the codec is and how many tokens per second it emits. Voice cloning turns a quality feature into a fraud vector.

Possible Solutions

  • Sample-level autoregression (WaveNet). Highest fidelity of its era; far too slow without distillation.
  • Two-stage TTS: text to mel spectrogram, then a neural vocoder (HiFi-GAN). Fast and controllable; two models to train and a mismatch between them.
  • End-to-end TTS (VITS). One model from text to waveform with a variational latent and adversarial training.
  • Codec language models (AudioLM, VALL-E, MusicGen). Tokenise audio with a neural codec (SoundStream, EnCodec) and generate tokens with a Transformer. Enables zero-shot voice prompting from a few seconds of audio.
  • Latent diffusion or flow for audio (for example Stable Audio, documented by Evans et al., 2024). Diffusion over a continuous audio latent; good for music and effects.

Why Raw Samples Are Hard: μ-law and WaveNet

WaveNet modelled 16 kHz audio sample by sample with dilated causal convolutions and a 256-way softmax over μ-law-companded values. μ-law allocates quantisation levels logarithmically, which matches loudness perception and gives more SNR per bit than linear quantisation on speech-like signals.

Neural Codecs: Residual Vector Quantisation

A neural codec is an encoder that turns a waveform into a low-rate sequence of vectors (75 per second for EnCodec at 24 kHz), a quantiser that turns each vector into codebook indices, and a decoder trained with reconstruction, spectral and adversarial losses. The quantiser is residual: codebook 1 quantises the vector, codebook 2 quantises what codebook 1 missed, and so on. More codebooks mean higher bitrate and fidelity; training with random codebook dropout makes one model serve several bitrates.

The runnable version below replaces the learned encoder with fixed 32-sample frames and learns each codebook with k-means. It is a simplified codec: no learned transform, no adversarial decoder. It keeps the part that matters for generation: audio becomes n_q parallel token streams whose count sets bitrate and quality.

n_q=2  codes=(2, 500)     8 kbps  SNR=24.95 dB
n_q=4  codes=(4, 500)    16 kbps  SNR=29.98 dB
n_q=8  codes=(8, 500)    32 kbps  SNR=34.58 dB
codebook 0 utilisation on test: 0.86 of 256 entries used
  • Each doubling of codebooks adds roughly 5 dB. Residual quantisation gives a smooth bitrate ladder from one trained model.

Vocoders and the Phase Problem

Two-stage TTS predicts a mel or magnitude spectrogram, which discards phase. The classical recovery is Griffin-Lim: alternate between enforcing the known magnitude and enforcing that the signal is a valid STFT. It reconstructs the magnitude well and the waveform poorly, which is exactly the gap neural vocoders were built to close.

STFT (122, 257); iSTFT with true phase: SNR=314 dB
Griffin-Lim iters=  0  spectral convergence=0.643  waveform SNR= -1.03 dB
Griffin-Lim iters= 10  spectral convergence=0.200  waveform SNR= -3.33 dB
Griffin-Lim iters= 50  spectral convergence=0.115  waveform SNR= -3.50 dB
Griffin-Lim iters=100  spectral convergence=0.068  waveform SNR= -3.59 dB
  • With the true phase the round trip is exact (SNR 314 dB is floating-point noise). The loss is entirely in phase.
  • Griffin-Lim reduces spectral convergence from 0.643 to 0.068, yet waveform SNR stays negative. Many different waveforms share a magnitude spectrogram; Griffin-Lim finds one with phase artefacts that listeners hear as a metallic, phasey sound.
  • This is why waveform SNR is the wrong metric for vocoders and why HiFi-GAN-style vocoders train with adversarial and multi-resolution spectral losses instead of sample-level MSE.

Evaluating Speech: Intelligibility by Round Trip

The cheapest automatic check for TTS is to transcribe the output with an ASR model and compute word error rate against the script. It catches skipped, repeated and wrong words, the characteristic failures of codec LMs. It does not measure naturalness or speaker similarity; those need MOS-style listening tests and a speaker-embedding similarity (Chapter 10).

WER=0.000  <- 'please confirm the delivery for tuesday at nine'
WER=0.125  <- 'please confirm delivery for tuesday at nine'
WER=0.125  <- 'please confirm the delivery for thursday at nine'

A dropped article and a wrong day score the same WER of 0.125, and only one of them changes the meaning of a delivery confirmation. Pair WER with a list of must-be-exact slots (dates, numbers, names) checked separately.

Video Generation

Video generation is image generation with a temporal consistency constraint and a bill multiplied by the number of frames, and then by attention's quadratic cost over all of them. This chapter demonstrates the consistency problem with the digits model (independent frames flicker; correlated noise does not), computes the spacetime token budget that dominates cost, compares full and factorised attention, and separates what is documented about large systems from what is inferred.

The Engineering Problem

A user asks for five seconds of a street at night. Generating each frame independently flickers: the car changes shape, the lights jump. A model that attends jointly over every patch of every frame has hundreds of thousands of tokens and will not finish inside a request timeout. You need a temporal coupling strong enough to keep identity and motion coherent, a token budget that fits the hardware, and metrics that catch both flicker and the subtler failure of smooth but wrong motion.

Possible Solutions

  • Per-frame image generation with shared seeds. Cheap; no motion model, so either static or flickering.
  • Inflate an image model with temporal layers (temporal convolutions and attention inserted between spatial layers), fine-tuned on video. Reuses image pretraining; used by Video Diffusion Models and Stable Video Diffusion.
  • Factorised space–time attention. Attend within each frame, then across frames at each position. Cost grows roughly linearly in frames.
  • Full spacetime transformers over latent patches. Joint attention over all spacetime tokens; best coherence, highest cost. The approach OpenAI's Sora report describes.
  • Autoregressive or chunked extension. Generate short clips conditioned on previous frames; long videos at the risk of drift.

Consistency Is a Property of the Noise and the Model

A clip of 16 frames sampled from the digits model, one frame at a time, with the same class, is the degenerate "video model". The only coupling between frames is the initial noise. Drawing it from an AR(1) process keeps each frame's marginal \mathcal{N}(0, I), so each frame is a valid sample, while neighbouring frames start close together.

z_f = \rho\, z_{f-1} + \sqrt{1-\rho^2}\;\xi_f,\qquad \xi_f\sim\mathcal{N}(0, I)\quad\Rightarrow\quad z_f\sim\mathcal{N}(0, I),\ \ \mathrm{corr}(z_f, z_{f-1}) = \rho
rho=0.0   flicker=0.472  identity=1.000  on-prompt=1.000
rho=0.9   flicker=0.201  identity=1.000  on-prompt=1.000
rho=0.98  flicker=0.108  identity=1.000  on-prompt=1.000
rho=1.0   flicker=0.000  identity=1.000  on-prompt=1.000
  • Independent noise (ρ = 0) gives flicker 0.472: every frame is a different handwriting of the same digit. A frame-level quality metric would score this clip as perfect.
  • ρ = 0.98 cuts flicker by more than four times while keeping identity and adherence at 1.000. ρ = 1 gives zero flicker and zero motion: a still image.
  • Identity stays at 1.000 in every row because the class condition is strong. In real video the equivalent failure is subtle: a person's face or a car's model changes slowly across frames while every frame remains "a person" or "a car". Identity metrics need an embedding that distinguishes instances, not classes.
  • Correlated noise alone cannot create coherent motion; it only interpolates between samples. Motion has to be learned from video, which is what the temporal layers of a real video model do. Noise priors of this kind were used to fine-tune image models for video (for example "Preserve Your Own Correlation", Ge et al., 2023).

Consistency Is Not Correctness
  • A clip can be perfectly smooth and physically impossible. Flicker metrics will be happy.
  • A clip can be right at the endpoints and broken in between. Score sampled middle frames, not just the first and last.
  • Frame-level FID rewards sharp frames and ignores flicker; FVD (Unterthiner et al., 2018) and human raters on motion quality are needed for video.
  • Independently sampled frames stitched together and described as video. The flicker number catches it.
  • Training at 16 frames and serving 128 without measuring identity drift over the clip.
  • Architecture claims about closed models inferred from how outputs look. Label the public report as documented and stop there.
  • Timeouts at the decoder: a 3D VAE decode of a long clip can exceed memory. Chunk it and budget it.

Evaluating Generative Media

A generated sample needs a distributional score, a condition-adherence score and a human score, and each of them can be gamed. This chapter measures the finite-sample bias of the Fréchet distance, shows a case where FD cannot tell a blob from eight modes while precision and recall can, catches a model that copies its training data, implements CLIPScore, fits a Bradley–Terry preference model with bootstrap intervals, and lays out speech and video metrics.

The Engineering Problem

Two checkpoints both "look good" on the five images the team picked. One has dropped half the modes. The other copies training images. A captioning score likes both because the captions are generic. You need a number that moves when the distribution moves, a number that moves when the prompt is ignored, a check for memorisation, and a human protocol for what no automatic metric sees, each with an uncertainty you can quote.

Possible Solutions

  • Likelihood (bits per dimension). Available for AR and flow models; poorly correlated with perceived quality.
  • Inception Score. Confidence and diversity of an ImageNet classifier; blind to the real data distribution.
  • Fréchet Inception Distance (Heusel et al., 2017). Distance between Gaussians fitted to features of real and generated images.
  • Precision and recall (Kynkäänniemi et al., 2019). Separate fidelity from coverage using k-nearest-neighbour manifolds in feature space.
  • CLIPScore (Hessel et al., 2021). Image–text cosine similarity in CLIP space for prompt adherence.
  • Human preference (pairwise comparisons, learned preference models such as ImageReward or PickScore). Closest to what users want; expensive and noisy.

Fréchet Distance and Its Bias

\mathrm{FD} = \|\mu_r - \mu_g\|^2 + \mathrm{Tr}\Big(\Sigma_r + \Sigma_g - 2\big(\Sigma_r^{1/2}\Sigma_g\Sigma_r^{1/2}\big)^{1/2}\Big)

FID is this formula on 2,048-dimensional Inception-v3 pool features. The means and covariances are estimated from samples, so FD is biased upward at small N, even between two samples from the same distribution.

Precision and Recall: Fidelity Versus Coverage

A single Gaussian cannot represent eight modes. Fitted to the Chapter 1 ring, it has the same mean and covariance as a wide round blob. Precision asks whether generated points fall on the real manifold (estimated by k-nearest-neighbour balls around real points); recall asks whether real points are covered by the generated manifold.

Memorisation

A model that reproduces training images can score an excellent FD. The check is the nearest-neighbour distance from generated samples to the training set, compared with the same distance from held-out real samples.

CLIPScore: Prompt Adherence

\mathrm{CLIPScore}(I, c) = w\cdot\max\big(\cos(E_I(I), E_T(c)),\ 0\big),\qquad w = 2.5

Hessel et al. proposed it for captioning; it is used in reverse to score whether an image matches its prompt. Libraries rescale differently: torchmetrics reports 100\cdot\max(\cos, 0). Always state which convention a number uses.

CLIPScore per pair: [2.5 1.5 0.  0. ]
torchmetrics-style, w=100: [100.  60.   0.   0.]

In this volume the probe accuracy of Chapters 5–7 plays CLIPScore's role: the condition is a class, the "text encoder" is the class label and the image encoder is the probe. The same limitations apply: the probe only sees what it was trained to see, and a generator can maximise it with stereotyped samples, as the guidance sweep showed (accuracy 1.000 at s = 5 with FD 14.0).

Human Preference with Uncertainty

Pairwise comparisons ("which image better matches the prompt?") are more reliable than absolute ratings. A Bradley–Terry model turns them into strengths, P(i \succ j) = \sigma(\beta_i - \beta_j), and a bootstrap over votes gives intervals.

model 0: true=+0.80  est=+0.87  95% CI=[+0.71, +1.09]
model 1: true=+0.30  est=+0.18  95% CI=[-0.04, +0.36]
model 2: true=+0.00  est=+0.06  95% CI=[-0.10, +0.23]
model 3: true=-1.10  est=-1.11  95% CI=[-1.32, -0.91]
  • With 600 votes the fit recovers the ranking, and the intervals are honest: models 1 and 2 (true gap 0.3) have overlapping intervals, so 600 votes cannot separate them. Chatbot-arena-style leaderboards for image and video models rest on exactly this computation.
  • Volume 10's sample-size arithmetic applies. Twenty preferences on a sheet of images do not justify shipping a style.

Serving and Safety

A generated image has a unit cost you can write as solver steps times a denoiser forward plus one decode, and a misuse case you cannot fix with a better FID. This chapter measures latency against steps and batch, prices an image under stated assumptions, builds a cache key that contains exactly what changes the pixels, signs a provenance manifest bound to the content bytes, and tests a watermark against the transformations that remove it.

The Engineering Problem

Interactive products want an image in a few seconds; batch products want a million images at the lowest cost; both run on GPUs billed by the hour. Every generation can be pointed at a person who did not consent to be depicted, at a living artist's style, or at a fake document. Regulators and platforms increasingly expect generated media to be labelled in a machine-readable way. The sampler does not know any of this. The serving layer and the policy must.

Possible Solutions

  • Fewer denoiser calls: tuned step counts, better solvers, flow models, distillation (Chapters 3 and 4).
  • Cheaper calls: latent spaces, smaller or quantised denoisers, batched guidance, compiled kernels, caching of repeated work.
  • Better utilisation: request batching, queues with deadlines, separate interactive and batch pools.
  • Safety controls before, during and after generation: prompt and input-image policy checks, concept filtering in training data, output classifiers, provenance manifests, watermarks, rate limits and audit logs.

Provenance: Signed Manifests

C2PA (the Coalition for Content Provenance and Authenticity) specifies a manifest attached to a media file: assertions about how it was made (for example "created by a generative model"), bound to a hash of the content and signed with the producer's certificate. It is not a watermark and not a detector; it is a signed claim that anyone can verify, and that anyone can strip. Documented OpenAI announced in 2024 that images generated with DALL·E 3 in ChatGPT and the API include C2PA metadata, and Adobe's Content Credentials are built on C2PA.

6db5cec227128800 2722ac4b6cc3d475
verify original:   ok
verify re-encoded: content changed
verify forged:     bad signature
  • Any byte change breaks the binding ("content changed"), including benign re-encoding by a social platform. Real C2PA handles legitimate edits by chaining manifests: each tool that edits the file adds a signed manifest referencing the previous one.
  • Changing a claim breaks the signature. Without the key, a forger cannot make "camera" verify.
  • Removing the manifest entirely leaves an unlabelled file that verifies as nothing. Provenance proves origin when present; its absence proves nothing. That gap is what watermarks try to fill.

Watermarks: Signals in the Content

An invisible watermark hides a keyed signal in the pixels (or audio samples, or the sampling process) so that it survives when metadata is stripped. The simplest is spread spectrum: add a keyed ±δ pattern and detect it by correlation, which for unmarked content is approximately standard normal.

PSNR of marked vs original = 32.0 dB
unmarked             z= -0.75  detected=no
marked               z= +4.74  detected=yes
wrong key            z= +0.66  detected=no

quantised to 8 bits  z= +4.74  detected=yes

crop+resize 2x       z= +1.64  detected=no

  • A one-pixel shift or a crop-and-resize destroys it (z below 2). Geometric robustness is the hard part; production schemes embed in transformed domains, in the latent, or in the initial noise (Tree-Ring watermarks), and train detectors with augmentation.
  • Documented Google DeepMind's SynthID watermarks outputs of its image, audio and video models; its internal method is only partly described publicly. Watermarks are evidence, not proof: an adversary with the detector or with regeneration through another model can often remove them.

Safety Architecture

What the Sampler Will Not Refuse on Its Own
  • Non-consensual imagery of real people, sexual content involving minors (which must be blocked absolutely and reported where law requires), impersonation for fraud, fake documents and election disinformation. These are policy decisions enforced before the job is queued, and in training data filtering, not properties of a checkpoint.
  • Style imitation of living artists and training-data licensing are legal and reputational questions; the data card and licence records are the artefacts. A generated sample does not launder them.
  • Output classifiers have false-negative rates you must measure on red-team sets, per category.
  • Documented The EU AI Act (Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, Article 50) requires providers of systems generating synthetic audio, image, video or text to mark outputs in a machine-readable format and make them detectable as artificially generated, with transparency obligations applying from August 2026. Check current guidance for your jurisdiction; this is not legal advice.

Volume Review

What You Learned

  • The five generative families differ in objective, calls per sample and characteristic failure. On one dataset: the VAE blurred (precision 0.714), the GAN skewed its mode masses (KL 0.038), and DDPM and flow matching matched an exact AR model (Chapter 1).
  • The forward process has a closed form x_t = \sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,x_0 + \sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,\epsilon; DDPM regresses ε; the loss is uninformative at both ends of the noise range and informative in the middle; the score-SDE view turns sampling into solving an ODE (Chapter 2).
  • DDIM reuses the same network with a deterministic update between any two noise levels. Quality has a cliff around 10 calls; stochasticity (η = 1) corrects errors at 50 steps; one-step distillation reached precision 0.892 against one-step DDIM's 0.120 (Chapter 3).
  • Flow matching regresses x_1 - x_0 along straight paths. At equal calls it beat DDIM at every budget; reflow cut path curvature from 0.70 to 0.0003 and made a one-step sampler; logit-normal time sampling is a measurable knob (Chapter 4).
  • Latent diffusion trades an autoencoder trained once for a much cheaper denoiser. The semantic ceiling arrived at latent width 8; the latent model beat a 4.4× larger pixel model (FD 5.80 versus 8.51); DiT cost is governed by token count (Chapter 5).
  • Cross-attention injects prompts; classifier-free guidance is one batched forward and one line of arithmetic; guidance raises adherence and lowers diversity with a U-shaped distance to data; zero-initialised layers add controls without disturbing the model (Chapter 6).
  • SDEdit strength has a sharp knee (6% to 87% edited between 0.2 and 0.4); replacement inpainting preserves known pixels exactly; prompt-to-prompt changes each patch in proportion to its attention on the swapped word (Chapter 7).
  • Audio generation runs on codec tokens: residual codebooks form a bitrate ladder, token rate is frame rate × codebooks, the delay pattern halves decoding steps, and phase is why vocoders are neural. Voice cloning needs consent (Chapter 8).
  • Video multiplies frames, tokens and attention; correlated noise trades flicker for motion; factorised attention is 30–60× cheaper at realistic sizes; public reports are documented at a high level only (Chapter 9).
  • FD is biased at small N and blind to multimodality in raw space; precision and recall separate fidelity from coverage; nearest-neighbour checks catch copying; CLIPScore has two conventions; preferences need intervals (Chapter 10).
  • Cost per image is steps × guidance factor × forward time plus decode, over utilisation; cache keys must contain every pixel-changing field; manifests prove origin when present; watermarks survive noise but not geometry; policy is the control (Chapter 11).

Important Architecture

Failure Modes

  • A schedule that never reaches noise; a parameterisation (ε, v, x0) or time convention mismatched between training and sampling.
  • A step count or guidance scale copied from another model; comparisons across different seeds.
  • Stochastic samplers behind a cache; guidance or sampler missing from the cache key.
  • A latent too small for the content, a missing scale factor, or an autoencoder swapped under a trained denoiser.
  • Guidance applied to the wrong tensor, the condition never dropped in training, or the paper's w used as Diffusers' s.
  • Edit strength with inverted semantics; masks broadcast wrongly; latent inpainting that alters unmasked pixels.
  • Audio judged by waveform SNR; dead codebook entries; codec LMs that skip words; voices cloned without consent records.
  • Independently sampled video frames; training at short clip lengths and serving long ones.
  • FID at small N or across incompatible pipelines; FD without precision and recall; no memorisation check; preference tests without intervals.
  • Half-denoised outputs returned on timeout; hashes called watermarks; detectors called proof; output classifiers as the only safety control.

Evaluation

  • 2-D toys: precision, modes covered, mode-histogram KL and MMD against held-out data, all at fixed noise so samplers are compared fairly.
  • Autoencoders: held-out reconstruction MSE and probe accuracy on reconstructions before any generator is trained.
  • Conditional generators: probe accuracy (adherence), feature-space FD against a real-vs-real floor, within-class diversity, swept over guidance scale.
  • Editing: adherence and distance to source at each strength; exact preservation of known pixels; seam statistics.
  • Audio: SNR only for codecs on synthetic signals; spectral convergence for phase recovery; WER round trip and slot checks for TTS; MOS and speaker similarity for release.
  • Video: flicker, identity consistency and on-prompt rate per frame; FVD and human motion ratings at scale.
  • Serving: latency by steps and batch, cost per image with stated assumptions, determinism tests for cache correctness, manifest and watermark verification under transformations.
