GPU Hardware for Engineers

Capacity plans fail when they treat a GPU as a generic accelerator. HBM bandwidth, interconnect topology and isolation features decide whether your batching math from Volume 12 is even physically possible.

The Engineering Problem

Procurement asks "how many H100s?" Product asks for interactive TTFT. Training wants all-reduce bandwidth. Inference wants HBM for KV. One SKU rarely optimises both. Without a hardware vocabulary you either over-buy or ship a latency SLO you cannot meet.

Possible Solutions

  • Buy the biggest GPU: raises peak FLOPs; does not fix a memory-bound decode path or a poorly packed MIG slice.
  • Rent managed tokens: defers hardware choice; you still need to know when self-hosting wins (Volume 12 crossover).
  • Match SKU to phase: training clusters for interconnect; inference pools for HBM and NVLink domains sized to tensor/pipeline parallel width.
Key Idea
  • Decode is usually memory-bandwidth bound: tokens/s tracks HBM GB/s more than peak TFLOPS.
  • Prefill is more compute-bound; long RAG prompts change the SKU economics.
  • Interconnect (NVLink, InfiniBand/RoCE) is a first-class capacity item for multi-GPU models.

  • HBM holds weights + KV. Concurrent sequences ≈ free HBM / bytes per token of KV (Volume 11). An "empty" GPU can still be full of cache.
  • NVLink couples GPUs inside a node (or rack domain) for tensor parallel. Crossing NVLink domains forces slower paths and can destroy TPOT.

Prefer specialised inference SKUs when

  • Models fit with room for KV at target concurrency.
  • You need many replicas more than peak TFLOPS.
  • $/MTok dominates over absolute tokens/s.

Prefer train-class GPUs when

  • You run large TP/PP/EP jobs or shared train+serve clusters.
  • Weights + KV need maximum HBM per device.
  • Your interconnect topology is already paid for.
  • Buying for FLOPs while decode is HBM-bound — utilisation looks high, goodput is not.
  • Ignoring driver / CUDA / engine matrix — a "free" GPU generation that will not boot your image.
  • Packing multi-tenant traffic on time-sliced GPUs and wondering why p99 TTFT exploded.

HBM Arithmetic Operators Use

A back-of-envelope still prevents bad POs. For a decoder-only model:

M_{\text{weights}} \approx \frac{P \cdot b}{8}\ \text{bytes}, \qquad M_{\text{KV}} \approx 2 \cdot L \cdot n_{\text{kv}} \cdot d_{\text{h}} \cdot s \cdot b_{\text{kv}} \cdot B
  • P parameters, b bits per weight, L layers, s sequence length, B concurrent sequences.
  • Leave headroom for fragmentation, CUDA context and engine scratch (often 10–20%).
  • If M_{\text{weights}} + M_{\text{KV}} exceeds HBM, no amount of "optimise the Python" fixes TTFT.
KV ~ 32.00 GiB for 64 seq @ 4k

Interconnect topology
  • Tensor parallel width should fit inside one NVLink domain when possible.
  • Pipeline parallel stages that cross slow links create bubbles; measure bubble fraction.
  • Expert parallel (MoE) adds all-to-all traffic — fabric congestion shows up as TPOT jitter.

For a 7B model at 4-bit weights, estimate HBM for weights plus KV for 64 concurrent 4k-context sequences. Does an L40S-class card clear the TTFT plan at your measured tokens/s, or do you need an H100-class card for memory alone?

Cloud AI Platforms

Cloud vendors sell the same physical ideas under different product names. The engineering choice is not logo preference — it is managed API versus managed endpoint versus raw GPU, and who owns the failure domain.

The Engineering Problem

A team can call a hosted chat API tomorrow, or stand up vLLM on rented GPUs in a week, or buy a managed endpoint that hides the engine. Each path locks different costs, data flows and operational toil. Mixing them without a boundary diagram produces three billings and one pager.

Names change; roles do not. Volume 12’s crossover calculator still applies: utilisation and cold-start tax dominate the managed-versus-raw decision.

Key Idea
  • Managed API: you buy tokens and SLAs; you do not schedule GPUs.
  • Managed endpoint: you bring weights; the platform runs the replica lifecycle.
  • Raw GPU: you own image, driver, engine, autoscaling and pages.
  • Neoclouds (specialist GPU clouds): often cheaper raw capacity, thinner platform services — budget for your own observability and IAM glue.

When each mode wins

  • Managed API: bursty traffic, many models, small ops team, data residency OK.
  • Managed endpoint: custom weights without wanting K8s GPU ops.
  • Raw / neocloud: steady utilisation, strict isolation, or unit economics past the crossover.

Neoclouds and Colocation

  • Specialist GPU clouds often undercut hyperscaler on-demand list prices for raw devices.
  • You typically bring more of the platform: identity glue, private networking, observability sinks.
  • Contract for interconnect topology, not only "8× GPU" — an 8-GPU VM with weak NVLink is a different product.
  • Exit plan: keep artefacts and the OpenAI-compatible edge portable (Volume 12).
Hybrid control plane
  • Route with forge.router capability profiles so a region outage can fail over to a managed API with a known quality floor.
  • Emit the same OTel attributes regardless of backend so dashboards do not fork.
Vendor lock-in
  • Keep the OpenAI-compatible contract from Volume 12 at the edge so backends can move.
  • Store artefacts (weights, prompts, eval sets) in your bucket with digests — not only in a vendor model garden.

Kubernetes for AI

Kubernetes schedules pods; AI workloads need GPU device plugins, gang scheduling for multi-GPU jobs, and storage that can feed multi-gigabyte weight pulls without stomping interactive replicas.

The Engineering Problem

A default Deployment with nvidia.com/gpu: 1 starts one replica. Training jobs that need eight GPUs together will partially schedule and deadlock. Inference replicas that pull 70 GB weights through the registry on every scale-up miss every TTFT SLO for minutes. CPU utilisation autoscaling still lies.

Possible Solutions

  • More replicas: does not fix gang deadlock or weight cold-start.
  • Bigger nodes: helps HBM; does not create a queueing policy.
  • GPU operator + device plugin + queue + cache: the production pattern.
Not executed

Manifests below are validated against documented Kubernetes and NVIDIA device-plugin interfaces. They were not applied to a live cluster in this volume’s authoring run. Use kubeconform / staging before production.

GPU Operators and Device Plugins

  • The NVIDIA GPU Operator (or equivalent) installs drivers, device plugin, DCGM exporter and often MIG managers.
  • Pods request nvidia.com/gpu (or MIG resource names). Without the plugin, the scheduler cannot count devices.
  • DCGM metrics (SM clocks, ECC, XID errors) belong next to your application Prometheus scrape.

Gang Scheduling and Queues

  • Gang scheduling: all pods of a multi-GPU job start together or none do. Without it, partial allocation starves the cluster.
  • Systems such as Volcano, Kueue or proprietary trainers implement queues with priorities, fair-share and quotas across teams.
  • Separate interactive inference node pools from batch train / offline pools so a research job cannot evict chat capacity.

Storage for Checkpoints and Weights

  • Object storage is source of truth; local NVMe / PVC caches absorb repeated pulls.
  • Pin weight digests in the registry (Volume 12). Rolling a bad digest is an ops incident.
  • Checkpoint writers need parallel filesystem or object multipart; do not NFS-mount a single filer as the only path for multi-node training.

Multi-Tenant Clusters

  • Namespaces per team are necessary but not sufficient — GPU quotas and PriorityClasses enforce fairness.
  • Noisy neighbours on shared node pools show up as TPOT jitter; dedicated pools cost more and simplify SLOs.
  • Chargeback needs the cost ledger labels (Chapter 9) propagated as pod labels / metric labels.
Disruption budgets
  • PodDisruptionBudgets on serve Deployments prevent cluster upgrades from draining all replicas.
  • Combine with terminationGracePeriodSeconds long enough to finish in-flight generations or shed cleanly.
Autoscaling reminder
  • Scale on queue depth / KV utilisation / tokens in flight — not mean CPU.
  • Account for cold start: image pull + weight load + CUDA graphs can be minutes.

Infrastructure as Code

Click-ops GPU clusters drift. Terraform (or equivalent) makes environments reviewable, promotable and recoverable — if secrets and state are handled like production data.

The Engineering Problem

Staging has a different CUDA driver, a hand-edited security group and an undocumented GPU quota. Production "works" until the next peer leaves. Reproducing an incident environment takes a week of archaeology.

Not executed

Terraform snippets are conceptual. They were not applied (terraform apply never ran). Run terraform plan in a sandbox account.

Terraform Concepts for AI Platforms

  • Modules: node pool, IAM role for nodes, artefact bucket, observability sink.
  • Workspaces / directories: dev → staging → prod with the same module versions and different variable files.
  • State: remote backend with locking; treat state as confidential.
  • Providers: cloud + Kubernetes + Helm common; pin provider versions.

Environment Promotion

  1. Merge module change → plan on dev.
  2. Apply dev; run synthetic canaries from forge.ops.canary.
  3. Plan/apply staging with production-like GPU SKUs if budget allows.
  4. Apply prod only with change windows funded by the error budget (Chapter 8).
Secrets
  • Never commit API keys, kubeconfigs or terraform.tfvars with credentials.
  • Inject secrets from a manager (cloud secret store / Vault) at runtime; Terraform may create the reference, not the plaintext value in state when avoidable.
  • Rotate provider tokens used by CI separately from human credentials.

State, Locking and Policy as Code

  • Remote state with locking prevents two applies corrupting the GPU node pool mid-rollout.
  • OPA / Sentinel / cloud organisation policies can deny public buckets for weight artefacts.
  • Store plan JSON beside the PR; require a human approval on destroy or quota increases.
Drift
  • Console edits to security groups create invisible drift — schedule terraform plan and page on non-empty diffs for prod.

Minimal checklist

  • Pinned module versions and provider constraints.
  • Plan artefacts stored with the PR.
  • Destroy protection on prod buckets and GPU reservations.
  • Drift detection job (plan on a schedule) that pages on unexpected changes.

Observability Foundations

You cannot page on vibes. Metrics, logs and traces answer different questions; OpenTelemetry GenAI semantic conventions give LLM calls a shared vocabulary across Forge services.

The Engineering Problem

Serve logs "done", Prometheus shows GPU util at 90%, and users still report empty replies. Without traces that bind gateway → router → model → tool, and without token usage on the span, you cannot tell timeout from refusal from upstream 429.

Three pillars
  • Metrics: aggregates for SLOs and autoscaling (Chapter 6).
  • Logs: sparse, structured events for incidents — not full prompts in cleartext by default.
  • Traces: causal graph of one request across services and tools.

Metrics and Dashboards

Prometheus histograms and Grafana boards turn Volume 12’s SLOs into pages someone can answer. The wrong metric (CPU, mean latency) produces confident autoscaling in the wrong direction.

The Engineering Problem

On-call sees "latency up". Which latency — TTFT or TPOT? Which tenant? Is KV full so new requests queue, or is decode batch size too large? A single red line without labels forces a guess during the incident.

Key Idea
  • Instrument TTFT, TPOT, queue depth, KV utilisation, goodput and token rates as separate series.
  • Alert on SLO burn, not on raw GPU utilisation alone.
  • Dashboards answer "user impact" first, "resource" second.

The Engineering Problem

Reliability Engineering

SLOs convert product promises into error budgets. Incidents, GPU failure modes and chaos tests spend that budget deliberately instead of discovering its size in an outage.

The Engineering Problem

Leadership wants 99.9% availability. Engineering ships daily. Without an error budget, every launch is either frozen or reckless. GPU nodes also fail differently from CPU web tiers: XID errors, ECC storms, stranded MIG slices and silent throughput collapse.

{'availability': 0.997, 'ttft_p95': 359.2, 'tpot_p95': 41.2, 'budget': 1.0000000000000009, 'burned': 3, 'remaining': -1.9999999999999991, 'breaches': ['availability', 'tpot_p95']}

In this toy hour, three failures already exhaust a 99.9% budget on 1 000 requests, and TPOT p95 slips the decode SLO — freeze risky launches until burn recovers.

Incident Response for Model Services

  1. Declare: user impact, blast radius (model, region, tenant).
  2. Mitigate: shed load, pin previous artefact, open breaker, fail over region.
  3. Diagnose: traces → engine metrics → DCGM / node events.
  4. Recover: restore capacity; verify goodput before closing.
  5. Learn: blameless postmortem with SLO burn attached.

Published fleet studies make these failures concrete. Weng et al. (2022) traced a production GPU cluster and found most jobs request fractional GPUs and leave them under-utilised, which is the case for sharing and MIG. Hu et al. (2024) characterised LLM development in a datacentre: pretraining jobs are few but dominate GPU time, evaluation jobs are many and short, and infrastructure faults are a leading cause of failed long jobs. MegaScale (Jiang et al., 2024) reports running training on more than 10,000 GPUs and depends on in-depth diagnostics, straggler detection and fast checkpoint recovery to keep MFU high. Kokolis et al. (2024) measure failure rates in large research clusters and show that the mean time to failure of a job falls roughly in proportion to the number of GPUs it spans. At 1,000+ GPUs, failures are routine events to engineer for, not exceptions.

Cost Management

Token bills and GPU hours are the same money in different costumes. FinOps for AI is attribution, utilisation discipline, and reserved/spot policy — reviewed monthly like any other large spend.

The Engineering Problem

Finance sees one cloud line item: "GPU". Product cannot tell which feature burned the budget. Engineering cannot tell whether to buy reservations or fix a retry bug. Without a ledger keyed by tenant, feature and model, every meeting is guesswork.

  • Product budgets are tokens or dollars per feature per day — enforced at the gateway.

Scaling

Horizontal and vertical scaling for LLMs are constrained by HBM, cold starts and stateful KV. Multi-region and disaster recovery inherit those constraints — copying a web-tier runbook wholesale will lie to you.

The Engineering Problem

Traffic doubles. You add pods. Half of them spend four minutes loading weights while the other half OOMs on KV. A region fails and DNS flips, but prefix caches are cold and TTFT collapses. Capacity without a drain and warm-up policy is theatre.

Possible Solutions

  • Vertical: bigger GPU / more HBM per replica — raises concurrency ceiling; does not remove single-node blast radius.
  • Horizontal: more replicas — needs queue-aware autoscaling and weight caches.
  • Disaggregation: separate prefill and decode fleets — operational complexity for large scale (research and early production systems).
Key Idea
  • Scale out on signals that track user SLOs: queue depth, KV headroom, goodput — not CPU.
  • Warm pools and cached weights are part of the capacity plan, not nice-to-haves.
  • Multi-region active-active needs sticky sessions or accept prefix-cache miss tax.

Multi-Region and Disaster Recovery

  • Active-passive: cheaper; failover RTO includes weight load unless standby is warm.
  • Active-active: route by latency and data residency; accept cache asymmetry.
  • Artefacts: replicate weight digests and config to each region’s bucket before cutover drills.
  • Stateful agents: externalise conversation state; do not rely on in-memory KV across regions.

Capacity reservations
  • Cloud GPU quota is not infinite at incident time. Hold reservations or committed use discounts sized to failover traffic, or document a degraded mode (smaller model, higher latency).
  • DNS failover without warm replicas — SLO burn during the "successful" DR test.
  • Scaling on GPU util while KV is the real constraint — thrashing pods.
  • Cross-region retries without hedged budgets — amplified cost and herd effects (Dean & Barroso).

Warm Pools and Drain

  • Keep minReplicas high enough that diurnal peaks do not wait on CUDA initialisation.
  • On scale-in, stop admitting new work, wait for in-flight generations, then terminate.
  • Pre-pull images and weights via DaemonSet or node cache — the silent half of autoscaling.

Security Operations

Volume 17 hardened the prompt channel. Security operations hardens the platform around it: IAM, network isolation, secrets, audit trails and supply-chain scanning for images and model artefacts.

The Engineering Problem

A leaked cloud key can pull every weight and every prompt log. A poisoned base image can backdoor the inference process. A flat VPC lets a compromised batch job scrape the chat namespace. Model files are large opaque blobs — teams skip scanning because "it is just weights".

Defence in depth
  • Least-privilege IAM for nodes, CI and humans — separate train and serve roles.
  • Private subnets for GPU nodes; egress allow-lists for model registries.
  • Secrets in a manager; short-lived tokens for CI; no long-lived keys in node user-data.
  • Sign and scan container images; pin digests in Deployments.
  • Scan model artefacts for embedded pickle / unexpected formats (Volume 17 supply tools).

Review and Next Steps

What You Learned

  • GPU choice is HBM, interconnect and isolation — not peak TFLOPS alone; train and infer SKUs diverge.
  • Cloud AI is three modes (managed API, managed endpoint, raw/neocloud) behind one product contract.
  • Kubernetes for AI needs GPU operators, gang scheduling, weight caches and queue-aware autoscaling.
  • IaC promotes environments; secrets and state are production data; Terraform/K8s examples stay plan-only until validated.
  • Observability splits metrics, logs and traces; OTel GenAI attributes bind tokens to request graphs.
  • Dashboards and alerts centre on TTFT, TPOT, KV, queue depth and goodput — not CPU vanity metrics.
  • LLM monitoring adds quality drift, input PSI, guardrail rates and cost anomalies.
  • Reliability is SLOs, error budgets, GPU-specific failure playbooks and staged chaos.
  • FinOps attributes spend to tenant/feature, then chooses reserved versus spot deliberately.
  • Scaling and DR must budget cold starts, cache miss tax and GPU quota in the failover region.
  • SecOps covers IAM, isolation, secrets and supply-chain pins for images and weights.

Failure Modes

  • Scaling on CPU while KV-bound — thrash without goodput.
  • Terraform apply to prod without plan review — silent security group drift.
  • Traces without token attributes — cost and latency cannot be joined.
  • Alerts on GPU util alone — misses empty 200s and quality cliffs.
  • Spot for interactive chat without drain — user-visible preemptions.
  • DR DNS cutover without warm weights — RTO fantasy.
  • Unpinned images/weights — unreproducible incidents.

Evaluation

  • Offline eval gates (Volume 10) still block digests; online canaries catch serving skew between staging and production.
  • Hold out a traffic sample for continuous eval; never train routers on the same slice you alert on.
  • Report quality with confidence intervals — a one-day dip may be noise.
  • Evaluate the monitoring itself: inject known faults (replica kill, quality drop, cost spike) in staging and measure time to detect and time to page. An alert that has never fired in a drill is untested code.
  • Check telemetry completeness: the fraction of requests with a trace, token counts and a cost-ledger entry should be ≥ 99%; gaps make attribution and incident review guesswork.
  • Review SLOs and cost attribution monthly against user-reported incidents and the cloud bill; if the bill and the ledger disagree by more than a few percent, the ledger is wrong.
