The Economics of Pretraining

Pretraining is a capital allocation problem. A fixed pile of FLOPs must be split between parameters and tokens, and the answer changes once you count the cost of serving the model afterwards.

The Engineering Problem

You have a budget: say 2,000 H100s for 30 days. You must commit, before the run starts, to a model size, a token count, a data mixture and a cluster layout. Mistakes are discovered weeks in, after millions of dollars are spent. The job of this chapter is to make that commitment with arithmetic rather than instinct.

The Compute Budget: C ≈ 6ND

  • For a dense decoder with N non-embedding parameters trained on D tokens, training FLOPs are approximately
    C \approx 6ND
  • Forward: each parameter participates in one multiply-add per token, so 2N FLOPs per token.
  • Backward: gradients with respect to activations (2N) and with respect to weights (2N), so 4N.
  • Ignored: attention score FLOPs, roughly 6 \cdot 2 \, n_{layer} \, n_{ctx} \, d_{model} per token (Kaplan et al. 2020). This is small when d_{model} \gg n_{ctx}/12 but matters for long-context training.
  • Activation recomputation (Chapter 5) adds another forward: 8ND of work for the same 6ND of useful FLOPs. MFU counts only the 6ND.
\text{wall-clock days} = \frac{6ND}{n_{GPU} \cdot \text{peak FLOP/s} \cdot \text{MFU} \cdot 86400}
Key Idea
  • Model FLOPs Utilisation (MFU) = achieved useful FLOP/s ÷ hardware peak. Good large dense runs reach ≈ 35–45 % on H100 in BF16; Llama 3 405B reported 38–43 % (Dubey et al. 2024).
  • Every inefficiency in later chapters (pipeline bubbles, exposed communication, recomputation, restarts) is a direct multiplier on the bill.

Inference-Aware Training

  • Chinchilla minimises training compute for a target loss. A deployed model's lifetime cost is 6ND + 2N D_{inf}, where D_{inf} is tokens served.
  • When D_{inf} \gg D, the optimum shifts to a smaller model trained on more tokens (Sardana and Frankle 2023). Llama 3 8B and most "small but strong" models follow this.
  • Over-training is not free: the loss curve flattens, so each extra token buys less. The trade is worthwhile only if the model is served at volume.
Trade-off
  • Over-training small models needs more unique data. Volume 5's dedup and quality filters decide whether 15T tokens exist that are worth training on.
  • Heavily over-trained models may be harder to fine-tune and quantise (anecdotal and model-specific; measure).

Evaluation and When to Use

  • Sanity-check any plan with three numbers: C, tokens per parameter, and GPU-days at a measured MFU from a short benchmark on the target cluster.
  • Budget 10–20 % extra for restarts, evaluation and ablations (≈; Llama 3 reported 419 unexpected interruptions in 54 days of 405B pretraining).

Scaling Laws

Scaling laws let you run cheap experiments and predict an expensive one. They are empirical curve fits, not physics, and their extrapolation error is the main risk they carry.

The Engineering Problem

You can afford dozens of runs at 1e17–1e20 FLOPs and exactly one at 1e24. You must choose the 1e24 model's size and token count from the small runs, and predict its loss well enough to know whether the run is healthy when it starts.

Kaplan et al. 2020

  • Loss as separate power laws in parameters, data and compute when the other factors are not bottlenecks:
    L(N) = \left(\frac{N_c}{N}\right)^{\alpha_N},\quad L(D) = \left(\frac{D_c}{D}\right)^{\alpha_D},\quad \alpha_N \approx 0.076,\ \alpha_D \approx 0.095
  • Compute-efficient allocation: N_{opt} \propto C^{0.73}, D_{opt} \propto C^{0.27}. Most extra compute goes to parameters; train large models and stop early.
  • This drove GPT-3's shape: 175B parameters on 300B tokens (≈ 1.7 tokens per parameter).

Hoffmann et al. 2022 (Chinchilla)

  • Three approaches (fixed model sizes with varied tokens; IsoFLOP profiles; a parametric fit) agree that parameters and tokens should scale roughly equally:
    N_{opt} \propto C^{a},\ D_{opt} \propto C^{b},\quad a \approx b \approx 0.5
  • Parametric form (Approach 3):
    L(N, D) = E + \frac{A}{N^{\alpha}} + \frac{B}{D^{\beta}},\quad E=1.69,\ A=406.4,\ B=410.7,\ \alpha=0.34,\ \beta=0.28
  • Result: ≈ 20 tokens per parameter. Chinchilla (70B, 1.4T tokens) beat Gopher (280B, 300B tokens) at the same compute.

Why Kaplan and Chinchilla disagree

  • Kaplan used a fixed learning-rate schedule length regardless of tokens, so short runs were not properly annealed and looked worse; this biased towards bigger models.
  • Kaplan counted non-embedding parameters and used smaller models; later analyses (Porian et al. 2024; Pearce and Song 2024) attribute much of the gap to embedding counting, last-layer compute and warmup/schedule choices.
  • Besiroglu et al. (2024) re-fitted Approach 3 from the paper's data and found the published parameters inconsistent with Approaches 1 and 2; treat Approach 3 constants as approximate.

What the output shows

  • With only 1 % noise over three decades, the fitted L_\infty is 1.815 against a true 1.69: the irreducible term is poorly identified from small runs.
  • The point forecast at 1e25 is off by ≈ 0.09 nats, and the 90 % band is ≈ 0.26 nats wide. That band is larger than the gap between many successive model generations.
  • Dropping L_\infty is catastrophic: the pure power law predicts 1.08 at 1e25 versus a true 1.85.

Limits and Extrapolation Risk

  • Range: extrapolating 5 decades from 3 decades of data. Fit on as wide a range as budget allows and hold out the largest run for validation.
  • Functional form: the choice between power law, power law plus constant, and broken power laws dominates the forecast (see above).
  • Hyperparameters: each small run must be tuned (or μP-transferred, Chapter 3). A mis-tuned small run bends the curve.
  • Data: laws are specific to the data mixture and tokeniser. Changing the mixture invalidates the fit; repeated data changes the D term.
  • Loss is not capability: downstream metrics can move abruptly while loss moves smoothly. Fit laws for task-level proxies separately and expect more noise.
  • Architecture changes: MoE, long context and different attention variants each need their own fit.
Production Practice
  • Use IsoFLOP profiles (Chinchilla Approach 2): for each of 5–8 budgets, train 5–9 model sizes; fit a parabola in \log N to find each minimum; fit a line through the minima. Llama 3 did this from 6e18 to 1e22 FLOPs to pick 405B for 3.8e25.
  • Llama 3 also mapped loss to downstream accuracy in a second fit, predicting ARC-Challenge accuracy for the 405B model from smaller runs (Dubey et al. 2024).
  • Use WSD schedules (Chapter 3) so one run with several cooldown branches yields several points on the curve.

Hyperparameters at Scale

At scale you cannot grid-search. Batch size is set by the gradient noise and the cluster, learning rate by a parameterisation that transfers from small models, and the schedule by how many times you want to stop.

Batch Size and the Critical Batch Size

  • McCandlish et al. (2018): below a critical batch size B_{crit}, doubling the batch nearly halves the steps (perfect data parallelism); above it, extra batch is wasted compute.
  • The simple noise scale estimates it from per-example gradients g_i with mean G and covariance \Sigma:
    B_{simple} = \frac{\operatorname{tr}(\Sigma)}{\lVert G \rVert^2}
  • Trade-off curve: \left(\frac{S}{S_{min}} - 1\right)\left(\frac{E}{E_{min}} - 1\right) = 1, where S is steps and E is examples processed. At B = B_{crit} you pay 2× the minimum of each.
  • Key property: B_{crit} grows as loss falls, because \lVert G \rVert shrinks while the per-example noise stays. This is why large runs ramp the batch size (Llama 3 405B: 4M tokens, then 8M, then 16M).
init          |G|^2=0.1495 tr(Sigma)=15.838 B_simple=   106.0
mid-training  |G|^2=0.0007 tr(Sigma)=3.823 B_simple=  5455.4
near optimum  |G|^2=0.0002 tr(Sigma)=3.558 B_simple= 21792.3
  • The noise scale rises ≈ 200× from initialisation to near the optimum. A batch that is wasteful at step 0 is efficient late in training.
  • In practice, estimate B_{simple} cheaply from gradient norms at two batch sizes (McCandlish et al. Appendix A) rather than per-example gradients.

μP Intuition

  • Under standard parameterisation (SP), the change in a layer's output after one Adam step grows with width, because the update \Delta W is correlated with the input x and \Delta W x sums \text{width} aligned terms.
  • μP (Maximal Update Parameterisation) rescales initialisation, per-layer LRs and the output multiplier so every layer's activations and updates stay \Theta(1) as width grows. The optimal LR, found on a small proxy, then transfers to the large model ("μTransfer").
width  |dW x| SP(lr fixed)  |dW x| muP(lr ~ 1/width)
  128              0.1114                  0.1114
  512              0.4087                  0.1022

 8192              6.5193                  0.1019
  • With a fixed LR, the output change grows linearly with width (64× wider, ≈ 58× larger update): a small-model LR would blow up the large model.
  • With LR ∝ 1/width the update stays constant. Full μP also changes the output-logit multiplier, attention scaling (1/d rather than 1/\sqrt{d}) and embedding LR; use the mup package or a published table rather than hand-rolling it.
Limits of μP
  • μP transfers across width. Transfer across depth needs extra scaling (e.g. depth-μP / residual branch scaling) and is less settled.
  • It does not transfer batch size, schedule length or weight decay. Those still need their own small sweeps.

Warmup-Stable-Decay (WSD)

  • Cosine decay requires fixing the total step count in advance; stopping early leaves the model un-annealed, continuing requires re-warming.
  • WSD holds the peak LR constant after warmup and decays (linearly, or to zero via 1-\sqrt{t}) over the last ≈ 10–20 % of steps. Hägele et al. (2024) show it matches cosine at equal compute.
  • Engineering benefit: fork cooldown branches from any stable-phase checkpoint. One long run yields a model at many token counts, which is exactly what a scaling-law fit needs.

Numerical Precision

Precision is the cheapest speed-up in training: halving the bits roughly doubles tensor-core throughput and halves memory traffic. It is also the easiest way to lose a run silently.

The Engineering Problem

FP32 training is numerically safe but leaves most of the hardware idle: H100 dense BF16 tensor throughput is ≈ 989 TFLOP/s versus ≈ 67 TFLOP/s for FP32 non-tensor maths (≈ 495 for TF32 tensor cores). You want low precision in the matmuls and full precision exactly where it matters.

Memory and Activation Management

Parameters are rarely what fills a GPU during training. Activations saved for backward grow with batch, sequence length and depth, and they are the first thing to trade for compute.

The Engineering Problem

A 70B model at sequence length 4,096 stores ≈ 1 GB of activations per layer per sequence in BF16 (Korthikanti et al. 2022 formula, below). Eighty layers do not fit on any GPU. Model-state sharding (Chapter 7) does not help: activations belong to the micro-batch, not the parameters.

Where the Memory Goes

  • Model states: BF16 weights (2 B/param), gradients (2 or 4 B/param), FP32 master + Adam m, v (12 B/param). ≈ 16–18 bytes per parameter before sharding.
  • Activations per transformer layer, sequence length s, micro-batch b, hidden h, heads a, tensor-parallel size t (Korthikanti et al. 2022, BF16):
    M_{act} = s\,b\,h\left(34 + \frac{5\,a\,s}{h}\right)\ \text{bytes (no parallelism)},\qquad \frac{s\,b\,h}{t}\left(34 + \frac{5\,a\,s}{h}\right)\ \text{with TP + sequence parallelism}
  • The 5as/h term is the attention score matrix; FlashAttention does not materialise it, leaving ≈ 34\,sbh/t.
  • Transient: communication buffers, allocator fragmentation, temporary workspace. Budget ≈ 5–10 % of HBM.

Activation Checkpointing

  • Chen et al. (2016): save only some activations (checkpoints) and recompute the rest during backward. With \sqrt{L} segments, memory drops from O(L) to O(\sqrt{L}) for one extra forward.
  • Full recompute per layer: store only each layer's input (2sbh bytes); cost ≈ +33 % FLOPs (one extra forward on top of forward + 2× backward).
  • Selective recompute (Korthikanti et al. 2022): recompute only the attention core (cheap FLOPs, big memory). Removes the 5as/h term for ≈ 2–4 % overhead; obsolete if FlashAttention already recomputes it.

  • Without checkpointing: 176 MiB saved = 16 MiB of weights referenced by the graph + ≈ 20 MiB activations per block (LayerNorm output, fc1 output, GELU output, residual) × 8.
  • Checkpointing every block keeps only the 8 block inputs (8 × 2 MiB = 16 MiB): an 11× reduction for ≈ 32 % more step time, close to the theoretical +33 %.
  • Gradients are identical: recomputation is exact when the block is deterministic. Dropout needs RNG-state replay (PyTorch does this by default with preserve_rng_state=True).
Saved-tensor hooks as a profiler
  • torch.autograd.graph.saved_tensors_hooks sees exactly what backward will need. The same hook pair can offload tensors to CPU (pack copies to pinned host memory, unpack copies back): this is how torch.autograd.graph.save_on_cpu works.
  • On GPU, confirm with torch.cuda.max_memory_allocated() and the memory snapshot tool (torch.cuda.memory._record_memory_history).

Data Parallelism

Data parallelism replicates the model, splits the batch, and averages gradients. It is the outermost layer of every parallel layout, and its all-reduce is the collective that the rest of the volume builds on.

The Engineering Problem

One H100 processes ≈ 9,000 tokens/s for a 7B model at 40 % MFU (0.4 \times 989\text{e}12 / (6 \times 7\text{e}9)). Fifteen trillion tokens would take about fifty years. You need thousands of workers computing one mathematically identical SGD step.

How DDP Works

  • Every rank holds the full model and optimiser; initial weights are identical (broadcast from rank 0 or same seed).
  • Each rank computes the loss on its B/N shard and backpropagates.
  • Gradients are summed with all-reduce and divided by N. Because the loss is a mean, the averaged gradient equals the full-batch gradient.
  • Each rank applies the same optimiser step, so replicas stay identical without communicating weights.

All-Reduce Algorithms and Cost

  • Alpha-beta model: sending n bytes costs \alpha + n\beta (latency plus inverse bandwidth). p ranks, message size n.
  • Ring: reduce-scatter (p-1 steps of n/p) then all-gather (p-1 steps):
    T_{ring} = 2(p-1)\,\alpha + 2\frac{p-1}{p}\,n\,\beta
    The bandwidth term is ≈ 2n\beta, independent of p: each rank sends and receives ≈ 2× the buffer. Latency grows linearly in p.
  • Tree / double binary tree (NCCL): O(\log p)\,\alpha latency with near-ring bandwidth; better for small messages and very large p.
  • Hierarchical: reduce-scatter inside the node over NVLink, all-reduce across nodes over InfiniBand, all-gather inside the node. Cross-node traffic shrinks by the GPUs per node.
  • Bus bandwidth (NCCL tests): \text{busbw} = \text{algbw} \cdot 2(p-1)/p. Compare busbw against link speed to judge efficiency.

Gradient Bucketing and Overlap

  • One all-reduce per parameter tensor pays \alpha hundreds of times; one all-reduce of everything after backward cannot overlap with compute.
  • PyTorch DDP (Li et al. 2020) groups gradients into ≈ 25 MB buckets in reverse layer order. An autograd hook fires when every gradient in a bucket is ready and launches an asynchronous all-reduce while backward continues on earlier layers.
  • Gradient accumulation: use no_sync() on all but the last micro-batch, otherwise you all-reduce every micro-batch.
  • Unused parameters (conditional branches, MoE experts not selected) never fire their hook; DDP hangs unless find_unused_parameters=True.
  • Different ranks take different control flow and issue collectives in a different order: deadlock. Collective order must be identical on every rank.
  • Data shards overlap because the sampler was not seeded per epoch: silent effective-batch reduction.
  • DP degree beyond B_{crit}/b_{micro} wastes compute (Chapter 3); DP alone cannot fit a model that does not fit on one GPU.

Sharded Data Parallelism

DDP keeps N identical copies of every byte of model state. ZeRO removes the redundancy by sharding optimiser state, gradients and finally parameters across data-parallel ranks, while keeping DDP's programming model.

The Engineering Problem

With mixed-precision Adam, a 7.5B model needs ≈ 120 GB of model states (16 bytes per parameter) before a single activation is stored. DDP replicates all 120 GB on every GPU. Tensor and pipeline parallelism can split it, but at the cost of code changes and communication on the critical path.

Why stages 1 and 2 are free in bandwidth

  • An all-reduce is a reduce-scatter followed by an all-gather (Chapter 6). ZeRO-2 stops after the reduce-scatter: each rank gets only the summed gradient shard it owns, updates that shard of the parameters, then all-gathers the updated parameters. Same bytes as DDP.
  • ZeRO-3 must all-gather parameters before each layer's forward and again for backward (they were freed), plus reduce-scatter gradients: ≈ 3\Psi versus 2\Psi, i.e. 1.5×.

PyTorch FSDP

  • Zhao et al. (2023): FSDP is ZeRO-3 as a PyTorch-native wrapper. Each wrapped unit (typically one transformer block) owns a FlatParameter sharded across ranks.
  • Forward: all-gather the unit's parameters, compute, free the unsharded copy. Backward: all-gather again, compute gradients, reduce-scatter them, free.
  • Prefetch: issue the next unit's all-gather while the current unit computes (forward and backward prefetch), hiding communication.
  • Hybrid sharding (HSDP): shard within a node (fast NVLink), replicate across nodes, and all-reduce across replicas. Limits all-gather traffic to NVLink.
  • FSDP2 (fully_shard) represents each parameter as a DTensor sharded on dim 0 instead of a flat buffer, which composes with tensor parallelism and makes per-parameter state dicts natural.
  • Mixed precision policy: parameters all-gathered in BF16, gradients reduced in FP32 or BF16, sharded master weights in FP32.

Simple Version

ZeRO-1 via torch.distributed.optim.ZeroRedundancyOptimizer around a DDP model. No model changes; ≈ 4× less optimiser memory at 4 ranks.

Production Version

FSDP2 per transformer block, HSDP across nodes, BF16 all-gather, FP32 reduce, prefetch, activation checkpointing per block, sharded distributed checkpoints (Chapter 11), and TP inside the node through the same DTensor device mesh.

Why It Is Complicated

Wrapping granularity trades memory against the number of collectives; prefetch depth trades memory against overlap; any rank-dependent control flow in a wrapped module deadlocks; and state dicts must be gathered or saved sharded without OOM on rank 0.

When ZeRO-3/FSDP is not enough
  • Activations are not sharded by ZeRO. At long sequence lengths they dominate, and you need TP/SP or context parallelism.
  • At thousands of GPUs, per-layer all-gathers across the whole DP group become latency-bound. Keep the sharding group small (HSDP) and add TP/PP.

Tensor and Sequence Parallelism

Tensor parallelism splits individual matrix multiplications across GPUs. It shrinks parameters, gradients and most activations per GPU, at the price of collectives inside every layer.

The Engineering Problem

A single transformer layer of a 70B model has ≈ 0.86B parameters and gigabytes of activations. Sharding across data-parallel ranks does not reduce per-micro-batch activation memory, and one GPU's matmul throughput caps per-layer speed. You want one layer to run on several GPUs at once.

Megatron-LM Column and Row Parallelism

  • Shoeybi et al. (2019). For the MLP Y = \text{GeLU}(XA)\,B:
  • Column-parallel A: A = [A_1, A_2]. Each rank computes \text{GeLU}(XA_i) independently: GeLU is elementwise, so no communication is needed between the two matmuls.
  • Row-parallel B: B = [B_1; B_2]. Each rank computes a partial Y_i = \text{GeLU}(XA_i)B_i; Y = \sum_i Y_i needs one all-reduce.
  • Two conjugate operators: f is identity in forward and all-reduce in backward (at the block input); g is all-reduce in forward and identity in backward (at the block output).
  • Attention is split the same way by heads: Q, K, V projections column-parallel, output projection row-parallel.

Communication Cost

  • Per layer (attention + MLP), TP needs 2 all-reduces in forward and 2 in backward, each of the full activation sbh elements (BF16: 2sbh bytes).
  • For a 70B layer (h = 8192, s = 4096, b = 1): one activation is 64 MiB; ring all-reduce over t = 8 sends ≈ 2 \cdot \frac{7}{8} \cdot 64 \approx 112 MiB per rank. Four per layer ≈ 450 MiB, 80 layers ≈ 35 GiB per step per micro-batch.
  • At NVLink ≈ 450 GB/s per direction (H100 NVLink 4) this is ≈ 0.08 s; over ≈ 50 GB/s InfiniBand per GPU it would be ≈ 0.7 s and fully on the critical path. Keep TP inside the NVLink domain.
  • TP communication cannot be hidden by bucketing: each all-reduce blocks the next matmul. Overlap needs collective-matmul decomposition (e.g. async TP / "TP comm overlap").

Sequence Parallelism

  • Korthikanti et al. (2022): LayerNorm and dropout regions are replicated under TP, so their activations (≈ 10sbh of the 34) are not sharded.
  • Split those regions along the sequence dimension. Replace each all-reduce with a reduce-scatter (into sequence shards) and an all-gather (before the next TP region). Same total bytes, since all-reduce = reduce-scatter + all-gather.
  • Result: all activations divided by t, giving the 34sbh/t term used in Chapter 10.

Context Parallelism and Ring Attention

  • For 128K+ context, even sbh/t is too large. Context parallelism (CP) shards the sequence across ranks for all layers, including attention.
  • Attention needs every key/value: ring attention (Liu et al. 2023) passes K/V blocks around a ring while each rank computes blockwise attention with online softmax, overlapping transfer with compute.
  • Llama 3 used CP = 16 with an all-gather of K/V (not a ring) during the 128K long-context phase (Dubey et al. 2024).
  • Causal masking unbalances work across sequence shards; assign each rank two chunks (one early, one late) to balance.

Pipeline Parallelism

Pipeline parallelism splits the model by layers into stages on different GPUs. Its communication is tiny and point-to-point, so it crosses slow links well; its cost is idle time, the bubble.

The Engineering Problem

TP must stay inside an 8-GPU node, and a 405B model does not fit in 8 GPUs even with ZeRO-1. You need to split the model across nodes, over InfiniBand, without an all-reduce on every layer.

Naive Pipelining and Micro-Batches

  • With p stages and one batch, only one stage works at a time: utilisation 1/p.
  • GPipe (Huang et al. 2018) splits the batch into m micro-batches, runs all forwards, then all backwards, and applies one synchronous update. Bubble fraction (idle over total, with t_f, t_b per micro-batch per stage):
    \text{bubble} = \frac{(p-1)(t_f + t_b)}{(m + p - 1)(t_f + t_b)} = \frac{p-1}{m+p-1}
  • GPipe's flaw: every stage stores activations for all m micro-batches before the first backward. Memory grows with m, and m must be large to shrink the bubble.
  • 1F1B (PipeDream-Flush; Narayanan et al. 2021): after p - s - 1 warm-up forwards, stage s alternates one forward with one backward. Same bubble, but at most p - s micro-batches in flight: memory bounded by p, not m.
  • Interleaved 1F1B (Narayanan et al. 2021): each GPU holds v non-contiguous chunks of layers; bubble becomes \frac{1}{v}\frac{p-1}{m} (relative to ideal time) at the cost of v\times more point-to-point messages.

Reading the timeline

  • Each two-character column is one time unit. F3 is the forward of micro-batch 3; B3b is its backward, which takes two units; . is idle.
  • The simulated bubble equals (p-1)/(m+p-1) exactly for both schedules: 1F1B does not reduce the bubble.
  • What 1F1B changes is memory: stage 0 holds 4 micro-batches (= p) instead of 8 (= m). At m = 64, GPipe needs 8× the activation memory of 1F1B for the same bubble.
  • Stage 0 holds the most activations under 1F1B. Its memory, not the average stage's, decides whether a layout fits (Chapter 10).

Practical Issues

  • Imbalanced stages: the embedding and the output projection plus loss (large vocabulary) make the first and last stages heavier. Llama 3 removed one layer from each (Dubey et al. 2024); others place fewer layers there.
  • Batch-size coupling: m = \text{global batch} / (\text{DP} \times b_{micro}). Increasing DP shrinks m and grows the bubble; early in training, when the batch is small, pipelines are least efficient.
  • Tied embeddings live on the first and last stages; their gradients must be all-reduced between those two stages.
  • Deadlocks: blocking send/recv must be ordered consistently or batched (batch_isend_irecv).
Zero-bubble schedules
  • Splitting backward into input-gradient (B) and weight-gradient (W) computation lets W fill bubbles, since W is not on the critical path to the previous stage (Qi et al. 2023, "Zero Bubble Pipeline Parallelism"). DeepSeek-V3's DualPipe also overlaps forward and backward communication.

Running Long Training Jobs

A frontier run is a distributed system that must stay correct for months while its hardware fails daily. Checkpointing, detection and recovery decide how much of the compute budget becomes model.

The Engineering Problem

Llama 3 405B pretraining saw 466 job interruptions in a 54-day snapshot, 419 of them unexpected, ≈ 78 % attributed to hardware (GPUs and HBM most often), yet achieved > 90 % effective training time (Dubey et al. 2024). At 16K GPUs, one interruption every ≈ 3 hours is normal. Recovery must be automatic and fast, and must never silently corrupt the run.

Distributed Checkpointing

  • Sharded: every rank writes only the state it owns (ZeRO/FSDP shards, TP slices, its pipeline stage). No rank gathers the whole model.
  • Atomic commit: write to a temporary location, fsync, then publish a manifest (or rename). A crash mid-save must leave the previous checkpoint valid.
  • Verifiable: checksums per shard; restore picks the newest checkpoint whose manifest and checksums verify.
  • Reshardable: restore on a different world size (a lost node, a new layout). Store logical tensor metadata (global shape, offsets), not rank numbers. This is what torch.distributed.checkpoint (DCP) does.
  • Complete: weights, optimiser state, LR-scheduler step, loss-scaler, every RNG state, and the data loader cursor (Volume 5's resumable sampler). Missing the data cursor re-trains on already seen data.
  • Asynchronous: copy state to host memory (fast), resume training, write to storage in the background (DCP async_save).

Checkpoint interval

  • Young's approximation: optimal interval \tau \approx \sqrt{2\,\delta\,M}, with \delta the time to save (blocking part only) and M the mean time between failures of the whole job.
  • Example: \delta = 30 s blocking, M = 3 h ⇒ \tau \approx \sqrt{2 \cdot 30 \cdot 10800} \approx 805 s, about every 13 minutes. Asynchronous saves shrink \delta and hence \tau.
  • Expected waste per failure ≈ \tau/2 + restart time (scheduler, NCCL init, loading). Restart time often dominates at scale; keep hot spares and cache checkpoints in local or host memory.

Failure Recovery

  • Detect hangs: collective timeouts (NCCL watchdog, TORCH_NCCL_ASYNC_ERROR_HANDLING), heartbeats per rank, and flight-recorder traces of the last collectives to find the rank that stalled.
  • Detect slow ranks: one throttled GPU slows every synchronous step. Track per-rank step time and all-reduce wait time.
  • Silent data corruption: faulty GPUs can produce wrong numbers without errors. Mitigations: periodic replay of a fixed batch comparing loss bit-for-bit across nodes, checksums on parameters across DP replicas (they must be identical).

Loss Spikes and Divergence

  • Causes observed in the literature: bad data batches, attention-logit growth, output-logit divergence, Adam's \epsilon being too large relative to \sqrt{v} for rarely updated parameters, and too-high LR for the scale (Wortsman et al. 2023; Chowdhery et al. 2022).
  • PaLM (Chowdhery et al. 2022) saw ≈ 20 spikes despite gradient clipping; they restarted from a checkpoint ≈ 100 steps before the spike and skipped ≈ 200–500 batches, which suggests the spikes arise from a data batch meeting a particular model state.
  • Mitigations in architecture: QK-norm, z-loss on the output logsumexp (PaLM used 10^{-4} \log^2 Z), no biases, careful initialisation, lower Adam \beta_2 (e.g. 0.95), smaller \epsilon.

Failure Modes

  • Scaling-law extrapolation without L_\infty, across a data or architecture change, or from un-tuned small runs.
  • Batch size far above B_{crit}; LR copied from a small model under standard parameterisation.
  • Comparing stable-phase and cooled-down checkpoints.
  • FP16 without loss scaling; BF16 accumulation in norms, losses or all-reduces; BF16 master weights.
  • Activation memory estimated for the average stage instead of pipeline stage 0.
  • Collective order differs across ranks: hangs. Unused parameters under DDP: hangs.
  • Gradient clipping by local norm under ZeRO/FSDP/TP.
  • Missing Megatron f operator: wrong input gradients with no error.
  • TP across nodes; pipeline micro-batches too few after raising DP.
  • Checkpoints without data cursor or RNG state; non-atomic saves; no checksums.
  • Silent data corruption on a faulty GPU; stragglers throttling the whole job.

Evaluation

  • Parallel correctness: every new parallel layout must match a single-device reference on a small model (loss curve over ≥ 100 steps within tolerance), as every listing here did.
  • Scaling fits: hold out the largest run; report prediction error and the band, not just the point estimate.
  • Efficiency: MFU, step-time breakdown, bubble fraction and exposed communication from profiles.
  • Reliability: effective training time (productive time ÷ wall time), mean time to recovery, checkpoint restore tested by actually restoring, including onto a different world size.
  • Model quality in-flight: held-out loss per domain and downstream evals at matched LR phase.

RESEARCH PAPER Scaling Laws for Neural Language Models Kaplan et al., 2020 · arXiv
Problem
No quantitative model of how loss depends on model size, data and compute.
Previous limitation
Scaling studied piecemeal, one factor at a time.
Approach
Hundreds of transformer runs over seven orders of magnitude; power-law fits in N, D and C.
Contribution
Smooth power laws; compute-efficient allocation favouring large models (N \propto C^{0.73}).
Practical impact
Justified GPT-3-scale models; made scaling experiments standard practice.
Limitations
Fixed-length LR schedules and non-embedding parameter counting biased the allocation.
RESEARCH PAPER Training Compute-Optimal Large Language Models Hoffmann et al., 2022 · NeurIPS
Problem
Were large models under-trained for their compute?
Previous limitation
Kaplan's allocation from runs without matched schedules.
Approach
Over 400 models (70M–16B); three estimation approaches; parametric L(N, D).
Contribution
Scale N and D equally; ≈ 20 tokens per parameter; Chinchilla 70B beats Gopher 280B.
Practical impact
Reset the industry towards more tokens per parameter.
Limitations
Ignores inference cost; Approach 3 constants later disputed (Besiroglu et al. 2024).
RESEARCH PAPER An Empirical Model of Large-Batch Training McCandlish et al., 2018 · arXiv

Gradient noise scale derived from a quadratic model of the loss.
