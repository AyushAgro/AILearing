When to Fine-Tune

Fine-tuning is the most expensive lever you can pull on a model's behaviour, and the one with the longest maintenance tail. Most teams that ask "should we fine-tune?" have a retrieval, prompting or evaluation problem. This chapter is a decision procedure.

The Engineering Problem

A support assistant built on a hosted model answers 71% of tickets acceptably. Product wants 90%. Someone proposes fine-tuning an open 8B model on 20,000 historical tickets. Before anyone rents GPUs, you must answer: which failures would fine-tuning fix, which would it make worse, and what would it cost to keep the fine-tuned model current for two years?

Classify the failure first

  • Knowledge gap: the model does not know a fact (a policy changed last week). Fine-tuning is a poor, lossy, stale-by-design database. Use retrieval (Volume 14).
  • Capability gap: the model cannot do the reasoning or transformation even with the facts in context. Use a stronger model, decomposition, or tools; fine-tuning rarely adds new reasoning ability at small data scale.
  • Behaviour gap: the model can do it but not reliably in your format, tone, length or policy. This is what SFT and preference tuning are best at.
  • Efficiency gap: a large model does it well but is too slow or expensive. Distil into a smaller model (Chapter 11).
  • Distribution gap: a whole domain's language (legal, clinical, a low-resource language, a codebase) is under-represented. Continued pretraining (Chapter 6).

Key Idea
  • Fine-tuning changes which outputs a model prefers far more than what it knows. LIMA (Zhou et al., 2023) argued that almost all knowledge comes from pretraining and alignment mainly teaches format: 1,000 curated examples were enough to make a 65B LLaMA a competitive assistant.
  • If a stronger model solves the task with the same prompt, the task is learnable, and distillation or SFT is plausible. If no model solves it, fine-tuning is unlikely to be the fix.

Cost and Maintenance

  • Compute is the small cost. A LoRA run on 10k examples of an 8B model costs tens of GPU-hours. Data curation, evaluation and re-runs cost engineer-weeks.
  • The base model will change. A fine-tune is pinned to one checkpoint. When a better base ships, you redo SFT, preference tuning and every evaluation. Budget for at least two re-runs per year.
  • You now own serving. A fine-tuned open model needs inference infrastructure (Volume 12), or a hosted fine-tuning API with its own lock-in.
  • Regression surface grows. Every post-training step can degrade unrelated abilities (safety, maths, multilingual). You need a regression suite, not just a target-task metric.

What Fine-Tuning Cannot Fix

  • Hallucination of facts: SFT on facts the model did not know tends to teach it to produce confident answers in that style, including for facts it still does not know. Gekhman et al. (2024) found that fine-tuning on new knowledge is learned slowly and increases hallucination.
  • Freshness: weights are a snapshot. Anything that changes weekly belongs in retrieval.
  • Access control: weights cannot enforce "user A may see document X". Put authorisation in the retrieval and tool layers (Volume 17).
  • Missing evaluation: without a test set you cannot tell whether the fine-tune helped. Build the evaluation first.
Trade-off
  • A fine-tuned small model can match a large prompted model on a narrow task at a tenth of the serving cost. The price is ownership: data pipeline, training, evaluation and serving. Decide on total cost of ownership over the model's lifetime, not on per-token price.

Supervised Fine-Tuning and Instruction Tuning

SFT is ordinary next-token training on demonstrations, with two details that decide whether it works: how conversations are serialised into tokens, and which tokens receive loss.

The Engineering Problem

A base model continues text; it does not answer questions. Asked "What is LoRA?", SmolLM2-135M may continue with more questions from a quiz. We want a model that, given a conversation, produces the assistant's next turn and then stops.

The Objective

Let a conversation be serialised into tokens x_1, \dots, x_T and let M be the set of positions belonging to assistant turns. SFT minimises

\mathcal{L}_{\text{SFT}}(\theta) = -\frac{1}{|M|} \sum_{t \in M} \log p_\theta(x_t \mid x_{<t})
  • This is Volume 4's cross-entropy, restricted to M. Prompt tokens still pass through the model as context; they just contribute no gradient.
  • In PyTorch and Hugging Face the restriction is implemented by setting label -100 (the ignore_index of F.cross_entropy) on every position outside M.
  • Normalisation matters: dividing by |M| per batch weights every token equally; dividing per sequence weights every example equally. With gradient accumulation, normalise by the total count of unmasked tokens across micro-batches, or short answers get over-weighted.

Chat Templates

  • A chat template is a Jinja program that turns a list of {"role", "content"} messages into a string with special tokens. It is part of the model's interface: training and inference must use the identical template.
  • Base checkpoints often ship no template. SmolLM2-135M's tokeniser has chat_template = None; its instruct sibling uses ChatML (<|im_start|>role\n…<|im_end|>), whose special tokens already exist in the base vocabulary as ids 1 and 2.
  • add_generation_prompt=True appends the header of an empty assistant turn. The model generates from there; this is also where loss masking should start.
  • The end-of-turn token must be in the loss. A model never trained to emit <|im_end|> never stops.

Multi-turn conversations
  • For multi-turn data, train on every assistant turn, not just the last: render once and mask by role span. Hugging Face templates that wrap assistant content in {% generation %} blocks let apply_chat_template(..., return_assistant_tokens_mask=True) return this mask directly.
  • Tool calls and tool results are separate roles. Train on the model's tool-call tokens; never train on tool outputs, which the model did not produce.

Packing

  • Problem: instruction examples are short (a few hundred tokens) and highly variable in length. Padding a batch to its longest member wastes most of the compute.
  • Naive packing concatenates examples into fixed-length rows separated by EOS. It fills the GPU, but causal attention now lets example 2 attend to example 1, which contaminates the context and teaches the model that unrelated conversations precede each turn.
  • Correct packing keeps a per-row list of document boundaries and uses a block-diagonal causal mask, with position ids reset at each boundary. FlashAttention's variable-length kernels (cu_seqlens) implement this without materialising the mask.
  • Loss normalisation changes with packing: a packed row mixes many examples, so per-sequence averaging is no longer per-example averaging.

Data Quality over Quantity

  • LIMA (1,000 examples) and the FLAN line (Wei et al., 2021; Chung et al., 2022) sit at opposite ends: a small amount of high-quality, diverse data aligns format; a large mixture of tasks with templates improves zero-shot generalisation across tasks.
  • Repeated low-quality demonstrations (refusal boilerplate, truncated answers, wrong code) are learned faithfully. SFT imitates; it does not filter.
  • Response style dominates: if 30% of demonstrations start with "Certainly!", so will 30% of outputs.

Catastrophic Forgetting

  • Full fine-tuning on a narrow distribution moves every weight towards that distribution. Abilities not exercised by the SFT data (other languages, maths, safety refusals) degrade.
  • Mitigations, in order of cost: lower learning rate and fewer epochs; LoRA (Biderman et al., 2024: "LoRA learns less and forgets less"); mixing in general instruction data; replay of pretraining data (Chapter 6); regularisers such as EWC (Kirkpatrick et al., 2017), rarely used at LLM scale.
  • Measure it: keep a frozen regression suite (general benchmarks, safety set, your previous tasks) and report deltas against the base for every checkpoint.
  • Template mismatch between training and serving (different system prompt, missing generation header): quality drops sharply with no error.
  • EOS not trained: the model rambles into a fake user turn.
  • Loss on prompts: the model learns to generate user questions and system prompts; prompt-injection text in data becomes behaviour.
  • Too many epochs on small data: verbatim memorisation of training answers; evaluation looks great if the test set leaks.

Instruction Data

The model you get is the data you train on. Instruction data pipelines have three sources, one filter stack and one legal question, and the filter stack is where most of the engineering goes.

The Engineering Problem

You need 20,000 high-quality demonstrations for an internal coding assistant. Human experts cost roughly USD 5–50 per example. A frontier model can generate 20,000 in an hour for a few hundred dollars. The generated set is cheaper, more consistent and more fluent, and it has duplicates, subtle errors, a narrow style and possibly licence restrictions.

The Self-Instruct Loop

  1. Seed a task pool with human-written tasks (175 in the paper).
  2. Prompt the model with 8 sampled tasks (6 human, 2 generated) to propose new instructions.
  3. Classify each as classification or generation; generate inputs and outputs (output-first for classification to avoid label imbalance).
  4. Filter: drop instructions with ROUGE-L > 0.7 to any existing one, keywords the model cannot handle (images, files), and malformed outputs.
  5. Add survivors to the pool and repeat.

Production filter stack

  • Correctness: execute generated code against tests; check maths with a solver; for prose, an LLM judge with a rubric plus human audit of a random sample.
  • Diversity: cluster prompt embeddings and cap examples per cluster; track instruction verb and topic distributions; over-sample the tail.
  • Difficulty: drop examples the base model already answers perfectly (they add little); keep hard-but-correct ones.
  • Decontamination: n-gram and embedding overlap against every evaluation set you will report.
  • Safety and PII: remove personal data, secrets and toxic content (Volume 5 filters apply).

Licence Hygiene

  • Many model providers' terms restrict using outputs to train competing models. Dataset licences (for example, non-commercial) propagate to your fine-tuned weights in practice even where the legal position is unsettled.
  • Keep a manifest per dataset: source, licence, generating model and its terms, date, filters applied, content hash. This becomes the "Training data" section of the model card (Chapter 12).
Data poisoning
  • Instruction data scraped from the web or user logs can contain deliberately planted trigger–behaviour pairs. A few hundred poisoned examples can install a backdoor that standard evaluations never trigger. Treat third-party instruction datasets as untrusted input (Volume 17).

Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning

Full fine-tuning updates every weight and stores a full copy per task. PEFT freezes the base and trains a small number of new parameters. LoRA won because it adds zero inference latency after merging.

The Engineering Problem

Full fine-tuning of an 8B model with Adam needs about 16 bytes per parameter (Volume 1): 128 GB before activations. Serving 50 customer-specific variants would need 50 copies of 16 GB of weights. We want per-task training that fits on one GPU and per-task artefacts measured in megabytes.

LoRA Derivation

For a frozen weight W_0 \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{out}} \times d_{\text{in}}}, LoRA constrains the update to rank r \ll \min(d_{\text{in}}, d_{\text{out}}):

h = W_0 x + \Delta W x = W_0 x + \frac{\alpha}{r} B A x, \qquad B \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{out}} \times r},\; A \in \mathbb{R}^{r \times d_{\text{in}}}
  • Hypothesis (Hu et al., 2021, building on intrinsic-dimension results): the update needed for adaptation has low intrinsic rank, even though W_0 is full rank.
  • Parameters: r(d_{\text{in}} + d_{\text{out}}) instead of d_{\text{in}} d_{\text{out}}.
  • Initialisation: A random, B = 0, so \Delta W = 0 at step 0. The fine-tune starts exactly at the base model. Initialising both randomly would perturb the model before training.
  • Scaling: \alpha / r keeps the effective learning rate of \Delta W roughly constant when you change r, so you can sweep rank without retuning the learning rate. rsLoRA argues \alpha/\sqrt{r} is the stable choice at high rank.
  • Merging: W = W_0 + \frac{\alpha}{r} BA is an ordinary dense matrix, so a merged model has no extra latency. Unmerging subtracts it again, which lets one server hot-swap adapters.

QLoRA and Memory-Efficient Training

LoRA removes optimiser state for the base weights, but the frozen weights themselves still occupy 2 bytes per parameter. QLoRA stores them in 4 bits and backpropagates through the dequantised values.

The Engineering Problem

A 65B model in bf16 is 130 GB of frozen weights alone. Dettmers et al. (2023) fine-tuned it on a single 48 GB GPU. That needs roughly 4 bits per weight with negligible quality loss, and a way to survive memory spikes from long sequences.

Three Ingredients

  • 4-bit NormalFloat (NF4): a 16-level code whose levels are quantiles of a standard normal, because pretrained weights are approximately zero-mean normal within a block. Each level is then used about equally often, which is information-theoretically efficient for normal data.
  • Double quantisation: the per-block fp32 scales are themselves quantised to 8 bits.
  • Paged optimisers: optimiser state lives in CUDA unified memory, so it pages to CPU RAM during gradient-checkpointing spikes instead of raising out-of-memory errors.

Computation is in bf16: each forward pass dequantises a weight block, multiplies, and discards the bf16 copy. Gradients flow through the dequantised weight to the LoRA parameters; the 4-bit codes never change.

Blockwise Absmax Quantisation

For a block w \in \mathbb{R}^{B} and a code book q_0 < \dots < q_{15} in [-1, 1]:

s = \max_i |w_i|, \qquad c_i = \arg\min_j \left| \frac{w_i}{s} - q_j \right|, \qquad \hat{w}_i = s \, q_{c_i}
  • Small blocks isolate outliers: one large weight only inflates the scale of its own block.
  • The cost is one scale per block: 32 bits per 64 weights is 0.5 extra bits per parameter.

Trade-offs of QLoRA
  • Training throughput is lower than bf16 LoRA (dequantisation on every forward and backward pass).
  • Merging a LoRA trained on NF4 weights into bf16 weights is not the model you trained: the adapter compensated for quantisation error that the bf16 base does not have. Either serve the quantised base with the unmerged adapter, or merge into the dequantised weights and re-evaluate.
  • The 4-bit base limits quality slightly; Dettmers et al. report NF4 plus double quantisation matching 16-bit LoRA on their benchmarks, but check on yours.

Continued Pretraining and Domain Adaptation

When a whole domain's language is missing, a few thousand demonstrations cannot supply it. Continued pretraining (CPT) runs the next-token objective on raw domain text, and the central engineering problem is not learning the domain but forgetting everything else.

The Engineering Problem

A general 7B model tokenises clinical notes poorly and misreads their abbreviations. You have 5B tokens of de-identified notes. Training on them alone improves clinical perplexity and degrades general reasoning, instruction following and safety behaviour, often invisibly until users find it.

Possible solutions and what each does not solve

  • Domain-adaptive pretraining (DAPT; Gururangan et al., 2020, "Don't Stop Pretraining"): continue on domain text. Does not preserve general ability on its own.
  • Task-adaptive pretraining (TAPT): continue on unlabelled text from the task distribution. Cheap; narrow.
  • Replay: mix a fraction of general pretraining data into the domain stream. The main forgetting defence; needs access to a representative general corpus.
  • LoRA CPT: forgets less but learns less; domain shifts large enough to need CPT often need high-rank updates.

Recipe

  • Re-warm, then re-decay the learning rate. A converged checkpoint sits at the end of a decayed schedule; jumping straight to a high rate causes a loss spike and forgetting. Ibrahim et al. (2024) show re-warming plus re-decaying plus replay matches retraining from scratch on the union at a fraction of the compute.
  • Peak learning rate lower than original pretraining (for example 1/3 to 1/10 of it); the higher it is, the more it learns and forgets.
  • Replay of 5–30% general data, drawn from the original distribution if you have it, or from a similar open corpus.
  • Tokeniser: extending the vocabulary for a new script or notation needs new embedding rows (initialised as the mean of their subword pieces) and more tokens to train them.
  • Then redo post-training: CPT degrades chat behaviour. Either re-run SFT and preference tuning, or CPT the base and merge (Chapter 11) with the original instruct model.

Forgetting Measurement in Practice

  • Perplexity on held-out general text (cheap, sensitive, weakly correlated with downstream quality).
  • Benchmark deltas on a fixed suite (knowledge, reasoning, code, multilingual), always against the pre-CPT checkpoint with paired comparisons (Volume 10).
  • Behavioural suites after re-running post-training: instruction following, refusals, format compliance.
  • Report forgetting as a budget with a number (for example, "no more than 1 point on the general suite"), and gate releases on it (Chapter 12).
  • No replay and a high re-warm learning rate: large general regressions that surface in production.
  • Domain data duplicated many times: memorisation of sensitive records (a privacy incident for clinical or legal data).
  • CPT on an instruct model: chat formatting and safety behaviour erode; you must redo alignment.

Reward Models

SFT can only imitate demonstrations. Humans are much better at comparing two answers than writing the best one, so preference learning starts by turning comparisons into a scalar reward.

The Engineering Problem

Labellers disagree on absolute quality scores (one person's 7 is another's 5), but they agree more often on "A is better than B". We need a function r_\phi(x, y) that scores any response to any prompt so that an optimiser can climb it.

Bradley–Terry Preference Model

Assume each response has a latent quality and the probability that a labeller prefers y_w over y_l is

P(y_w \succ y_l \mid x) = \sigma\big(r(x, y_w) - r(x, y_l)\big) = \frac{\exp r(x, y_w)}{\exp r(x, y_w) + \exp r(x, y_l)}

Maximum likelihood on a dataset of comparisons \mathcal{D} = \{(x, y_w, y_l)\} gives the reward-model loss

\mathcal{L}_{\text{RM}}(\phi) = -\,\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l) \sim \mathcal{D}} \left[ \log \sigma\big(r_\phi(x, y_w) - r_\phi(x, y_l)\big) \right]
  • Only differences matter: r is identified up to an additive constant per prompt. Normalise rewards (for example, zero mean over a reference set) before using them in RL.
  • Architecture: the SFT model with the LM head replaced by a scalar head reading the final token's hidden state. InstructGPT used a 6B reward model for a 175B policy.
  • With K responses ranked per prompt, InstructGPT trained on all \binom{K}{2} pairs from one prompt in the same batch, to avoid overfitting from correlated pairs.

Training and Evaluating a Reward Model

Synthetic setting: responses are 16-dimensional feature vectors, a hidden utility vector defines true quality, and labels are sampled from a Bradley–Terry labeller. Feature 0 is "length", which the true utility ignores. In the second run the labellers have a length bias.

  • Accuracy ceiling: labels are noisy, so even the true utility agrees with only 88.7% of held-out labels. The unbiased RM reaches 87.8%, close to that ceiling. Real RMs on human data score 65–75% because inter-labeller agreement is itself around 70–75% (InstructGPT reports ~73%).
  • Bias is learned faithfully: with length-biased labellers, the RM's training accuracy is higher, its accuracy against true utility drops by 8 points, and it pays +6.2 reward for length alone. A policy optimised against this RM will get longer. This is the documented length bias of RLHF (Singhal et al., 2023).

Reward Hacking and Over-Optimisation

  • A reward model is a proxy trained on a finite distribution. The policy is an optimiser that searches for inputs where the proxy is wrong, in regions the RM never saw.
  • Gao et al. (2022) measured this with a large "gold" RM standing in for humans: as the policy moves away from its initialisation (measured by d = \sqrt{D_{\text{KL}}(\pi \| \pi_{\text{ref}})}), proxy reward rises monotonically while gold reward rises, peaks and falls. The gold curve fits d(\alpha - \beta \log d) for RL and d(\alpha - \beta d) for best-of-n; larger RMs over-optimise later.
  • Typical hacks: length, lists and headings, sycophancy (agreeing with the user), confident tone, hedging, and exploiting known RM blind spots such as repeated phrases.

Mitigations

  • KL penalty to the reference policy (Chapter 8), and early stopping on a held-out gold signal (human evaluation or a stronger judge).
  • Reward-model ensembles; penalise disagreement.
  • Explicit length control: length-normalised rewards, length-matched pairs, or a length penalty.
  • Refresh the RM with preferences collected on the current policy's outputs (iterated or online RLHF).

Evaluating Reward Models

  • Held-out pairwise accuracy, broken down by category (chat, safety, reasoning, code). RewardBench (Lambert et al., 2024) is a public example.
  • Calibration: does \sigma(r_w - r_l) match observed agreement rates?
  • Adversarial probes: pairs differing only in length, formatting, or a planted factual error.
  • Best-of-n curves: sample n responses, pick the RM's favourite, and check with humans or a gold judge whether quality actually rises with n.

RLHF with PPO

RLHF optimises the policy against the reward model while a KL penalty keeps it near the SFT model. PPO is the optimiser InstructGPT used; it works, and it is the most operationally demanding step in post-training.

The Engineering Problem

We have an SFT policy and a reward model. Sampling many responses and keeping the best (best-of-n) already improves quality but multiplies inference cost by n forever. We want to move that improvement into the weights without letting the policy exploit the reward model.

The KL-Regularised Objective

\max_{\pi_\theta} \; \mathbb{E}_{x \sim \mathcal{D},\, y \sim \pi_\theta(\cdot \mid x)} \big[ r_\phi(x, y) \big] \;-\; \beta \, \mathbb{E}_{x \sim \mathcal{D}} \Big[ D_{\text{KL}}\big(\pi_\theta(\cdot \mid x) \,\|\, \pi_{\text{ref}}(\cdot \mid x)\big) \Big]
  • \pi_{\text{ref}} is the frozen SFT model. The KL term keeps outputs in the region where the RM is trustworthy and where the language stays fluent.
  • This objective has a closed-form optimum (derived in Chapter 9): \pi^*(y \mid x) \propto \pi_{\text{ref}}(y \mid x) \exp(r(x, y) / \beta). PPO approximates it with sampled gradients; DPO exploits the closed form directly.
  • In practice the KL is applied per token: the reward at token t is -\beta(\log \pi_\theta(y_t \mid \cdot) - \log \pi_{\text{ref}}(y_t \mid \cdot)), and the RM score is added at the final token.
  • InstructGPT's PPO-ptx also adds a pretraining log-likelihood term to reduce regressions on public NLP benchmarks (the "alignment tax").

PPO's Clipped Surrogate

With probability ratio \rho_t(\theta) = \pi_\theta(a_t \mid s_t) / \pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(a_t \mid s_t) and advantage estimate \hat{A}_t (Schulman et al., 2017):

\mathcal{L}^{\text{CLIP}}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_t \Big[ \min\big( \rho_t(\theta) \hat{A}_t,\; \operatorname{clip}(\rho_t(\theta), 1 - \epsilon, 1 + \epsilon)\, \hat{A}_t \big) \Big]
  • Samples are expensive (autoregressive generation), so PPO reuses each batch for several gradient epochs. Clipping removes the incentive to move \rho outside [1-\epsilon, 1+\epsilon], a cheap trust region.
  • For LLMs, a state is the prompt plus tokens so far, and an action is the next token. Advantages come from a learned value head via generalised advantage estimation (GAE):
\delta_t = r_t + \gamma V(s_{t+1}) - V(s_t), \qquad \hat{A}_t = \sum_{l \ge 0} (\gamma \lambda)^l \, \delta_{t+l}

Reading the result

  • β = 0 reproduces Gao et al.'s over-optimisation curve in miniature: true reward rises to 2.67 at iteration 15, then collapses to −1 once the policy finds the loophole, while proxy reward keeps climbing. Without a gold signal you would ship the worst model.
  • β = 1: true reward rises from 0.84 to about 1.27–1.36 while KL stays near 0.5 nats and the loophole stays at ~5% probability. The PPO result tracks the closed-form optimum (true 1.24, KL 0.51), which verifies the implementation.
  • β = 0.5 is in between: half the mass drifts to the hack. β is not a regulariser you set once; it decides which point on the proxy–gold curve you land on.
  • The initial KL is 0.06, not 0: it is measured after the first update.
  • GAE: with \gamma = 1 the sparse final reward propagates back to every token through the value baseline. Omitted here: the value model is trained with a regression loss on the returns, which doubles the trainable state.

Why RLHF Is Operationally Hard

  • Memory: four models (policy, reference, reward, value), two with optimiser state. For a 70B policy that is hundreds of GB before activations; teams share weights (value head on the RM), offload the reference, or use LoRA so the reference is "adapter disabled".
  • Generation in the loop: rollouts are autoregressive and dominate wall-clock time; they need an inference engine (vLLM-style) with weights synchronised from the trainer every step.
  • Hyperparameter sensitivity: KL coefficient (or adaptive KL target), clip range, value-loss coefficient, reward normalisation and whitening, GAE \lambda, generation temperature, number of epochs. Huang et al. (2024) list dozens of implementation details needed to reproduce OpenAI's results.
  • Instability: reward and KL can diverge suddenly; entropy collapse produces repetitive outputs; value-model errors produce noisy advantages.
  • Evaluation lag: proxy reward always rises, so every checkpoint needs a gold-signal evaluation to catch the turn-over.
Beyond PPO
  • GRPO (Shao et al., 2024) drops the value model and uses the mean reward of a group of samples for the same prompt as the baseline, the same trick as our batch-mean baseline. It is the workhorse of RL with verifiable rewards for reasoning models (Volume 9).
  • RLOO (REINFORCE leave-one-out) and plain REINFORCE with good baselines work well for LLMs because the "environment" is a single step with a terminal reward.

Direct Preference Optimisation and Variants

DPO removes the reward model and the RL loop. It observes that the KL-regularised objective has a closed-form optimum, inverts it to express the reward in terms of the policy, and fits that implicit reward to preferences with a classification loss.

The Engineering Problem

Chapter 8's pipeline needs four models, online generation and a dozen fragile hyperparameters. We want the same objective (maximise preference-consistent reward, stay close to the reference) with a training loop as simple as SFT.

Derivation

Step 1: the optimum of the KL-constrained objective. For a fixed prompt x, rewrite the objective from Chapter 8:

\max_\pi \; \mathbb{E}_{y \sim \pi}[r(x,y)] - \beta D_{\text{KL}}(\pi \| \pi_{\text{ref}}) = -\beta \, \mathbb{E}_{y \sim \pi}\left[ \log \frac{\pi(y \mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y \mid x) \exp(r(x,y)/\beta)} \right]

Define the partition function Z(x) = \sum_y \pi_{\text{ref}}(y \mid x) \exp(r(x,y)/\beta) and the normalised distribution \pi^*(y \mid x) = \pi_{\text{ref}}(y \mid x) \exp(r(x,y)/\beta) / Z(x). Then the objective equals

-\beta \, D_{\text{KL}}\big(\pi \,\|\, \pi^*\big) + \beta \log Z(x)

which is maximised exactly when \pi = \pi^*, since Z(x) does not depend on \pi.

Step 2: invert for the reward. Taking logs of \pi^*:

r(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi^*(y \mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y \mid x)} + \beta \log Z(x)

Step 3: substitute into Bradley–Terry. Only reward differences for the same prompt appear, so \beta \log Z(x) cancels:

P(y_w \succ y_l \mid x) = \sigma\left( \beta \log \frac{\pi^*(y_w \mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w \mid x)} - \beta \log \frac{\pi^*(y_l \mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l \mid x)} \right)

Step 4: maximum likelihood over the policy. Parameterise \pi^* by \pi_\theta (Rafailov et al., 2023):

\mathcal{L}_{\text{DPO}}(\theta) = -\,\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l)} \left[ \log \sigma\left( \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_w \mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w \mid x)} - \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_l \mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l \mid x)} \right) \right]
  • \hat{r}_\theta(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y \mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y \mid x)} is the implicit reward: "your language model is secretly a reward model".
  • The gradient is a weighted likelihood update:
\nabla_\theta \mathcal{L}_{\text{DPO}} = -\beta \, \mathbb{E}\Big[ \sigma\big(\hat{r}_\theta(x, y_l) - \hat{r}_\theta(x, y_w)\big) \big( \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_w \mid x) - \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(y_l \mid x) \big) \Big]
  • Pairs the model already ranks correctly get small weights; wrongly ranked pairs get weights near 1. Unlike unlikelihood training, the reference ratio stops the model from degenerating.
  • \log \pi_\theta(y \mid x) is the sum of token log-probabilities over the response only: exactly the masked quantity from Chapter 2.

  • Both log-probabilities fall: DPO only constrains the margin. In practice the chosen log-probability often decreases too, moving mass to responses in neither set. Monitor logps/chosen; add an SFT term on chosen responses (as in RPO) if it collapses.
  • Length exploitation: summed log-ratios favour longer chosen responses when preferences are length-biased (SimPO's motivation).
  • Off-policy data: pairs generated by another model teach the policy about responses it would never produce. Generate candidates from the SFT policy itself, then label.

Offline versus Online Preference Learning

  • Offline (vanilla DPO): a fixed dataset of pairs. Cheap and stable; the policy drifts away from the data distribution as it trains, so later updates learn from increasingly off-policy pairs.
  • Online or iterative: sample new pairs from the current policy each round, label with humans, an RM or an AI judge, and repeat DPO. Closes much of the gap to PPO in published comparisons at a fraction of the complexity.
  • Choice: start with offline DPO on on-policy (SFT-generated) pairs; move to iterative DPO when gains plateau; use PPO or GRPO when you have a reliable, cheap reward (verifiable tasks, Volume 9).

AI Feedback and Constitutional Methods

Human preference labels are slow, expensive, inconsistent and sometimes harmful to collect (labellers reading toxic content). If a strong model can judge responses against written principles, it can replace most of the human labels.

The Engineering Problem

You need 200,000 preference labels on harmlessness and helpfulness, refreshed every time the policy changes. At USD 1 per human comparison and a week's turnaround, iterative preference learning is infeasible.

Constitutional AI

Bai et al. (2022, "Constitutional AI") replace human harmlessness labels with a list of natural-language principles (the constitution), in two stages.

  • Supervised stage: the model critiques its own harmful answer ("Identify ways this response is harmful…"), revises it, and is fine-tuned on the revisions. This removes the most egregious behaviour cheaply.
  • RL stage (RLAIF): an AI model compares pairs of responses against a principle; its probabilities (soft labels) train the preference model. Human labels remain for helpfulness in the original work.
  • Outcome reported: a less evasive model that was more harmless at equal helpfulness than an RLHF baseline; chain-of-thought in the judge improved label quality.

RLAIF

  • Lee et al. (2023) compared RLAIF with RLHF on summarisation and dialogue: human raters preferred them at similar rates, and even a judge the same size as the policy helped.
  • Direct RLAIF: skip the reward model and query the LLM judge for a score during RL. More expensive per step; no RM staleness.
  • The same machinery powers modern synthetic pipelines: generate n candidates from the policy, score them with a judge or RM, keep best and worst as a DPO pair (UltraFeedback, Zephyr's distilled DPO; Tunstall et al., 2023).

Writing Principles

  • Specific and checkable beats aspirational: "Do not provide step-by-step synthesis routes for weapons" is testable; "Be good" is not.
  • Each principle becomes a judge prompt; sample one principle per comparison to cover the set.
  • Keep principles, judge prompts and judge model versions under version control: they are training data.
  • Judge bias propagates: position bias, verbosity bias and self-preference (a judge favouring its own family's style) become the policy's behaviour. Swap positions and average; audit with humans.
  • Over-refusal: harmlessness principles without helpfulness counterweights produce evasive models. Measure over-refusal on benign prompts that look risky.
  • Collapse of diversity: optimising many models against the same judges homogenises style.
  • Judge exploitation: the policy learns what the judge rewards, exactly as with a learned RM (Chapter 7).

Distillation and Model Merging

Two ways to get a model without another expensive training run from preferences: copy a bigger model's behaviour into a smaller one, or combine several fine-tuned models' weights into one.

The Engineering Problem

A 70B model meets the quality bar but costs 10× too much to serve. Separately, you have three LoRA fine-tunes of the same 8B base (SQL, support tone, German) and want one model that does all three without multi-adapter serving.

Knowledge Distillation

Hinton et al. (2015) train a student on the teacher's softened distribution. With temperature T, p^{(T)} = \operatorname{softmax}(z / T):

\mathcal{L}_{\text{KD}} = \alpha \, T^2 \, D_{\text{KL}}\big(p_{\text{teacher}}^{(T)} \,\|\, p_{\text{student}}^{(T)}\big) + (1 - \alpha)\, \mathrm{CE}\big(y, p_{\text{student}}^{(1)}\big)
  • Soft targets carry "dark knowledge": the relative probabilities of wrong answers encode similarity structure that a one-hot label discards.
  • The T^2 factor compensates for gradients of the soft term shrinking as 1/T^2, so \alpha keeps its meaning when you change T.
  • For LLMs: logit distillation applies this per token (needs the teacher's logits and a shared tokeniser); sequence-level distillation (Kim & Rush, 2016) is SFT on teacher-generated text, which works across tokenisers and closed APIs.
  • Forward KL (above) is mode-covering; reverse KL D_{\text{KL}}(p_s \| p_t) is mode-seeking and is preferred by on-policy distillation methods (the student generates, the teacher scores its tokens).

Model Merging

Fine-tunes of the same base stay in a connected low-loss region, so their weights can often be combined arithmetically.

  • Weight averaging / model soups (Wortsman et al., 2022): average several fine-tunes of the same task with different hyperparameters; often beats the best single run on accuracy and robustness.
  • Task arithmetic (Ilharco et al., 2022): a task vector is \tau_i = \theta_i - \theta_0. Add them to combine skills, subtract to remove behaviour:
\theta_{\text{merged}} = \theta_0 + \lambda \sum_i \tau_i
  • TIES-Merging (Yadav et al., 2023) targets interference between task vectors in three steps: trim each \tau_i to its top-k% magnitude entries (default 20%); elect a sign per parameter from the summed magnitudes; disjoint merge, averaging only the entries that agree with the elected sign.

  • Log per run: base model revision hash, tokeniser and chat template hash, dataset manifest hashes, full config, library versions, seed, hardware, and the evaluation report.
  • Track the right training metrics: masked token count per step (SFT); reward mean and std, KL, entropy, clip fraction and value loss (PPO); rewards/chosen, rewards/rejected, margins, accuracy and logps/chosen (DPO).
  • Save adapters and optimiser state separately; adapters are megabytes and cheap to keep for every checkpoint.

Model Cards and Release

Mitchell et al. (2019) proposed model cards as standard release documentation. For a post-trained model, include:

  • Lineage: base model and revision; every post-training stage in order (CPT, SFT, DPO, merge, quantisation) with configs.
  • Training data: dataset manifests, licences, synthetic data generators and their terms, filters, decontamination against the reported evaluations.
  • Intended use and out-of-scope use, including the failure classes from Chapter 1 that fine-tuning does not fix.
  • Evaluation: task metrics with confidence intervals, regression deltas against the base, safety and over-refusal results, known weaknesses.
  • Serving requirements: chat template, stop tokens, recommended sampling settings, quantisation format.

Release

  • Shadow traffic, then a canary percentage, with online metrics compared to the incumbent (Volume 12).
  • Keep the previous model hot for instant rollback; adapters make this cheap.
  • Feed production failures back into the evaluation set before the next training run.
Fine-tuning erodes safety
  • Qi et al. (2023) showed that fine-tuning an aligned model on a few dozen adversarial examples, or even on benign data, can remove safety behaviour. Every fine-tune, including customer fine-tunes on a platform, needs its own safety regression gate.

Volume Review

What You Learned

  • Fine-tuning fixes behaviour, format and efficiency gaps; it is a poor fix for knowledge freshness, access control and missing capabilities.
  • SFT is masked next-token loss: labels -100 on everything except assistant tokens, end-of-turn token included, with an identical chat template at training and serving.
  • Data quality, diversity and decontamination matter more than volume; synthetic data needs a filter stack and licence tracking.
  • LoRA trains \Delta W = \frac{\alpha}{r}BA with B = 0 at initialisation, and merges into a dense weight with zero serving overhead.
  • QLoRA stores the frozen base as NF4 with blockwise absmax scales and double quantisation (4.127 bits per parameter), putting 70B fine-tuning on one 80 GB GPU.
  • Continued pretraining needs replay and learning-rate re-warming, and forgetting must be measured against a budget.
  • Reward models fit Bradley–Terry likelihoods; they learn labeller biases faithfully, and optimising against them over-optimises.
  • RLHF maximises reward minus \beta·KL, with PPO's clipped surrogate and four models; \beta decides where you land on the proxy–gold curve.
  • DPO follows from the closed-form optimum \pi^* \propto \pi_{\text{ref}} e^{r/\beta}; IPO, KTO, ORPO and SimPO each fix one of its weaknesses.
  • AI feedback (RLAIF, Constitutional AI) replaces most human labels, and imports the judge's biases.
  • Distillation transfers behaviour through soft targets; merging combines fine-tunes of a shared base, and TIES reduces interference.

Important Architecture

Failure Modes

  • Fine-tuning to add knowledge that belongs in retrieval.
  • Chat-template mismatch, double label shift, untrained end-of-turn token, loss on prompts.
  • Naive packing with cross-document attention.
  • Merging a LoRA trained on an NF4 base into bf16 weights without re-evaluation.
  • Continued pretraining without replay; catastrophic forgetting found by users.
  • Reward models learning length and style biases; reward hacking with β too small.
  • DPO with deterministic or off-policy pairs; chosen log-probabilities collapsing.
  • AI-judge biases (position, verbosity, self-preference) becoming policy behaviour.
  • Distilled models that imitate style but not capability.
  • Shipping on a headline win rate without forgetting, safety and over-refusal gates.

Evaluation

  • Build the evaluation set before the training set, and decontaminate training data against it.
  • Target metrics with paired confidence intervals against the incumbent.
  • Regression suites with explicit forgetting budgets (general ability, safety, previous tasks).
  • For preference tuning: gold-signal checks (humans or a stronger judge) on every checkpoint, KL to reference, response length distribution.
  • Over-refusal on benign-but-risky-looking prompts; format validity for structured outputs.
  • Serving parity after merging and quantisation.

RESEARCH PAPER LoRA: Low-Rank Adaptation of Large Language Models Hu et al., 2021 · ICLR
Problem
Full fine-tuning of every weight for each downstream task is storage- and compute-heavy at LLM scale.
Previous limitation
Adapters added depth and latency; prefix tuning was hard to optimise and slow at inference for long prefixes.
Approach
Freeze the pretrained weight W and learn a low-rank update \Delta W = BA injected into attention (and later FFN) projections; merge at serving time.
Contribution
Matched full fine-tuning quality with orders-of-magnitude fewer trainable parameters; no extra inference latency when merged.
Practical impact
Default PEFT method in open-source stacks; foundation of QLoRA multi-adapter serving.
Limitations
Rank and target-module choices matter; can underfit relative to full FT on some domains (Biderman et al. 2024).
RESEARCH PAPER QLoRA: Efficient Finetuning of Quantized LLMs Dettmers et al., 2023 · NeurIPS
Problem
Fine-tuning a 65B model needed more than 780 GB of GPU memory in 16-bit.
Previous limitation
4-bit quantisation was used for inference; training through quantised weights lost quality.
Approach
Freeze the base in NF4 with blockwise scales and double quantisation, backpropagate into LoRA adapters in bf16, and page optimiser state.
Contribution
65B fine-tuning on a single 48 GB GPU matching 16-bit fine-tuning quality on their benchmarks; the Guanaco models.
Practical impact
Made fine-tuning large open models accessible on single GPUs; standard in bitsandbytes, PEFT and TRL.
Limitations
Slower than bf16 LoRA; adapters are tied to the quantised base; evaluation relied partly on GPT-4 judgements.
RESEARCH PAPER Training Language Models to Follow Instructions with Human Feedback Ouyang et al., 2022 · NeurIPS
Problem
Pretrained LMs next-token predict well but do not reliably follow user instructions or stay helpful and harmless.
Previous limitation
SFT alone underused preference signal; pure RL without a KL constraint drifted from the pretrained distribution.
Approach
InstructGPT pipeline: SFT on demos → Bradley–Terry reward model on rankings → PPO with KL penalty to the SFT policy (PPO-ptx mixes pretraining gradients).
Contribution
Showed labelers preferred a 1.3B InstructGPT model over a 175B base GPT-3; established the three-stage RLHF recipe.
Practical impact
Template for ChatGPT-era alignment stacks and for open RLHF libraries.
Limitations
Operationally heavy (four models); proxy reward over-optimisation; preferences reflect a narrow rater pool.
RESEARCH PAPER Direct Preference Optimization: Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model Rafailov et al., 2023 · NeurIPS
Problem
RLHF with PPO is complex, unstable and expensive: a reward model, on-policy sampling and four models in memory.
Previous limitation
Treating preference learning as RL required explicit reward modelling and policy-gradient estimation.
Approach
Use the closed-form optimum of the KL-constrained objective to reparameterise the reward in terms of the policy, then fit Bradley–Terry preferences with a binary cross-entropy loss.
Contribution
An RL-free preference optimisation method matching or beating PPO on sentiment control, summarisation and dialogue.
Practical impact
Became the default open-source preference tuning method and spawned IPO, KTO, ORPO and SimPO.
Limitations
Offline and off-policy by default; overfits deterministic preferences; only constrains the margin, so chosen likelihood can fall.
RESEARCH PAPER Constitutional AI: Harmlessness from AI Feedback Bai et al., 2022 · arXiv
Problem
Harmlessness from human labels alone is expensive and exposes raters to toxic content.
Previous limitation
HH-RLHF needed many human preference labels for both helpfulness and harmlessness.
Approach
Write a constitution of principles; critique-and-revise with an AI; train on AI preference labels (RLAIF) for harmlessness while keeping human labels for helpfulness.
Contribution
Showed AI feedback can replace much of the harmlessness labelling while remaining competitive with human RLHF.
Practical impact
Made principle-driven self-critique and RLAIF standard tools in post-training.
Limitations
Constitution quality and judge biases still matter; not a substitute for red-teaming.
RESEARCH PAPER TIES-Merging: Resolving Interference When Merging Models Yadav et al., 2023 · NeurIPS
Problem
Averaging fine-tuned checkpoints (or task vectors) often destroys specialised skills through sign interference.
Previous limitation
Model soups and naive task arithmetic ignore conflicting update directions across tasks.
Approach
Trim low-magnitude parameters, elect a consensus sign per coordinate, then merge only agreeing disjoint updates.
Contribution
Higher multi-task retention than averaging or untrimmed task arithmetic on several multitask suites.
Practical impact
Practical recipe for combining LoRA/domain adapters without full joint retraining.
Limitations
Hyperparameters (trim rate) matter; does not create capabilities absent from the parents.
