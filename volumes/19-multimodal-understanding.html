Multimodal Problem Framing

A multimodal system is a Transformer whose sequence was built from more than one kind of sensor, or a pipeline of models that pass each other text. The engineering problem is alignment: which image region, which audio frame and which word are allowed to influence each other, at what point in the network, and what that costs in tokens.

The Engineering Problem

A support tool must answer questions about scanned invoices, screenshots and short voice notes. Stuffing each page in as 4,000 image tokens works and blows the budget. Running OCR first is cheap and drops the stamp in the margin. A vision–language model sees the stamp and sometimes invents a total. Audio has the same fork: transcribe and send text, or send audio tokens to a model that hears tone. The team needs a vocabulary for these choices, and a way to price them, before it picks a model.

Possible Solutions

  • Pipeline of specialists. OCR, ASR or a detector turns media into text; a text LLM reasons. Cheap, debuggable, each stage measurable. It does not solve anything the specialist drops: layout, handwriting, a stamp, sarcasm in a voice.
  • Early fusion. Tokenise every modality into one sequence and train one network from the start. The joint model can use fine correspondences. It demands huge paired data and a shared tokeniser design; you cannot bolt it onto an existing LLM.
  • Late fusion. Separate encoders, combine their outputs at the end (a cosine score, a vote, a learned gate). Easy to swap one encoder, and it is how CLIP retrieval works. It does not let a word point at a region: nothing inside either encoder sees the other modality.
  • Projection (prefix) fusion. Freeze a vision encoder, map its tokens into the language model's embedding space with a small layer, and prepend them to the text. This is the LLaVA shape. Every image token then costs context and attention in every LM layer.
  • Cross-attention fusion. The language model attends into a separate bank of vision tokens through inserted cross-attention layers (the Flamingo shape). The image does not occupy context positions, and new layers must be trained.

Alignment Is the Real Problem

Every fusion strategy is an answer to "how do representations from two sensors end up in a space where they can be compared or combined?".

  • Global alignment: a whole image and a whole caption land near each other (CLIP, Chapter 4). Enough for search and zero-shot labels.
  • Token alignment: a word can attend to the patch that supports it (projection and cross-attention VLMs, Chapter 5). Needed for "what is the total in the bottom-right box?".
  • Temporal alignment: an audio frame or a video frame is tied to a timestamp (CTC in Chapter 9, moment retrieval in Chapter 10).
  • Symbolic alignment: a detector emits boxes with ids that the LLM cites (Chapter 7). Checkable by code, which is its advantage.
Key Idea
  • Modality is a decision about the sequence you build, not a separate kind of intelligence.
  • Choose the weakest alignment that answers your questions. Global alignment is cheap; token alignment is expensive in tokens; symbolic alignment is cheap and auditable but only as good as the specialist.
  • A system that OCRs and then calls an LLM is multimodal. It is not a multimodal model. Volume 1's distinction still holds.

The Token Bill Comes First

A square image of side H with patch P becomes (H/P)^2 tokens before a class token or any special markers. Text of a typical page is a few hundred tokens. The ratio is the reason document systems still OCR.

224px patch 16: 196 tokens
448px patch 16: 784 tokens
1024px patch 16: 4096 tokens
1024px patch 32: 1024 tokens

What the counts say

  • Doubling the side length quadruples the tokens if the patch stays fixed. 224 → 448 is 196 → 784. Attention cost then scales with the square of that, which is why high-resolution pages hurt.
  • Doubling the patch (16 → 32) cuts a 1024-pixel page from 4096 tokens to 1024. You pay in detail: a digit that occupied a few pixels may now share a patch with the line next to it.
  • These counts are the ViT grid only. A class token, a newline token per row, token merging, or a thumbnail-plus-tiles scheme (Chapter 3) changes them. Quote the scheme when you quote a number. Chapter 11 turns vendor formulas into the same kind of function.

Where the Image Tokens Go: Prefix Versus Cross-Attention

Projection and cross-attention differ in where the image tokens sit, and so in what they cost. With a prefix, the I image tokens join the T text tokens in every self-attention layer. With cross-attention, text attends to image tokens only in the inserted layers, and the image never occupies a context position.

\text{prefix: } L\,(T+I)^2 \qquad\qquad \text{cross-attention: } L\,T^2 + \tfrac{L}{k}\,T\,I
image_tokens=576: projection (prefix)=24.6M, cross-attention=4.3M
image_tokens=2304: projection (prefix)=217.0M, cross-attention=8.4M
  • At 576 image tokens (a LLaVA-1.5-sized grid) the prefix design scores about 6× more pairs. At 2,304 tokens (four tiles of that grid) the gap is 26×, because the prefix cost is quadratic in T+I and the cross-attention cost is linear in I.
  • Pairs are not the whole bill. The prefix also grows the KV cache by I positions per layer for the rest of the conversation. Cross-attention keeps a separate, fixed-size image cache.
  • So why is prefix fusion the common open-model design? It needs no new LM layers, works with any decoder, and handles text interleaved with images for free. Cost is the price of that simplicity. Resamplers (Chapter 5) and token merging attack I directly.

Gated cross-attention does no harm at initialisation

Flamingo (Alayrac et al., 2022) inserts new cross-attention layers into a frozen LM and multiplies their output by \tanh(\alpha) with \alpha = 0 at the start. The frozen LM therefore behaves exactly as before on the first step, and training opens the gate gradually.

Vision Transformers

A Vision Transformer is a Transformer on a sequence of patches. Once the image is a sequence, Volume 2 applies. The new pieces are the patch embedding, the class token, the fact that position embeddings are no longer optional, and an appetite for data that convolutional networks do not have.

The Engineering Problem

Convolutional nets bake in locality: a pixel mostly sees its neighbourhood until you stack many layers. That bias is a gift on small datasets and a constraint when you have so much data that you would rather let the model learn which pixels matter. You also want one architecture that can sit next to a language model, share its tooling and kernels, and emit a sequence the LM can attend over, without a second theoretical stack.

Possible Solutions

  • A convolutional net. Strong locality and translation structure; excellent sample efficiency. The feature map is not yet a sequence a language model can attend to. You can flatten it, and you have reinvented the patch grid with extra bias.
  • A ViT (Dosovitskiy et al., 2020). Cut the image into non-overlapping patches, linearly embed each, add positions, run a standard Transformer. Data-hungry. The sequence interface and the scaling behaviour are the point.
  • A hierarchical transformer (windowed attention, merged patches). Cheaper at high resolution, closer to a CNN's pyramid. More machinery. Use it when the token bill in Chapter 1 is the blocker, not when you are learning the idea.
  • Hybrid: a CNN stem producing the tokens for a Transformer. The original ViT paper evaluated this too. Useful at small data; muddier to scale.

From Pixels to a Sequence

N = \frac{H}{P}\cdot\frac{W}{P}, \qquad \mathbf{z}_0 = [\,\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{cls}};\, \mathbf{x}_p^1\mathbf{E}; \ldots; \mathbf{x}_p^N\mathbf{E}\,] + \mathbf{E}_{\mathrm{pos}}

\mathbf{E} is a matrix of shape (P^2 C,\ D). The class token is a learned vector that is allowed to attend to every patch; the vector at its position after the stack is what classification reads. It is not a summary you can assume is faithful for retrieval. Many later systems drop it and pool the patch tokens instead, and VLMs usually discard it and keep the patch tokens.

Reading the run

  • An 8×8 RGB image with patch 4 is four patches. Plus the class token the sequence length is 5, and the width is 16. Shape (2, 5, 16) is two images through that block.
  • The parameter split is the formula: patch embed 4\cdot4\cdot3\cdot16 = 768, class token 16, positions 5\cdot16 = 80, four attention matrices 4\cdot16^2 = 1024. There is no MLP in this block, so do not compare 1888 with a production ViT-B count.
  • The top and bottom halves of the image were swapped, which swaps two patches. Without positions, the patch token follows the pixels: the paired outputs differ by 10^{-18}. With positions, the same pairing differs by 0.05. Position embeddings are what make "the patch in the corner" different from "the same pixels moved". Volume 2 showed the same fact for words.

The production patch embedding is a strided convolution

Every ViT implementation you will read uses Conv2d(C, D, kernel_size=P, stride=P) instead of a reshape and a matmul. They are the same linear map; the convolution is simply the fastest kernel for it. Proving that is a good check that you understand the patch order.

The residual of 4\times10^{-7} is float32 round-off from a different summation order. Get the permutation wrong (for example (1, 2, 3, 0)) and the error is order 1: the map is still linear, but it reads channels as columns.

A Trainable ViT in PyTorch

Test: Inductive Bias Against Data

The ViT paper's central empirical claim is that a ViT trained on ImageNet alone trails comparable ResNets, and overtakes them when pretrained on much larger data (JFT-300M) Documented (Dosovitskiy et al., 2020). The claim can be reproduced in miniature: classify the shape (square, circle, triangle, cross) of one coloured object at a random position in a 16×16 image, with 500 or 5,000 training images and the same number of optimiser steps.

n=  500 vit params= 71620 train_acc=1.000 test_acc=0.787 sec=5.8
n=  500 cnn params= 56580 train_acc=1.000 test_acc=0.993 sec=9.2
n= 5000 vit params= 71620 train_acc=0.984 test_acc=0.985 sec=7.2
n= 5000 cnn params= 56580 train_acc=1.000 test_acc=1.000 sec=11.3

What the table says

  • At 500 images both models memorise the training set (train accuracy 1.000). The CNN generalises (0.993); the ViT does not (0.787). Translation equivariance and locality are knowledge the CNN did not have to learn.
  • At 5,000 images the ViT closes to within 1.5 points (0.985). The gap is a data gap, not a capacity gap: the ViT has more parameters.
  • The CNN is still better here. The ViT paper's crossover required hundreds of millions of images. What you should take from a laptop run is the direction of the effect, not the crossover point.
  • The ViT was faster per epoch at this size, because its 16-token sequence is short. At 1024 pixels with patch 16 that stops being true (Chapter 1).

Image Encoders and Self-Supervision

A ViT with random weights is a tensor function. An encoder is that function after it has been forced to represent images well without your labels. This chapter builds a masked autoencoder, shows honestly where it does and does not help at laptop scale, explains the self-distillation trick behind DINOv2, and handles the problem every production encoder meets on day one: images that are not the size it was trained on.

The Engineering Problem

You do not have a million labelled invoices. You do have a pile of unlabelled page images and a few hundred labelled ones. Training a classifier from scratch overfits the labels (Chapter 2 showed a ViT at 500 images). You want an encoder whose features are already useful, then a small head or a projection on top. And the pages arrive at 1275×1650 while the encoder was trained at 224×224.

Possible Solutions

  • Supervised pretraining on a broad labelled set, then fine-tune. Simple. The label set leaks into what the encoder notices, and it needs labels at scale.
  • Masked autoencoders (He et al., 2021). Hide most patches, encode the visible ones, predict the missing pixels. No labels. Cheap per step because the encoder skips masked tokens. Features are strong after fine-tuning and weaker under a frozen linear probe.
  • Self-distillation (DINO and DINOv2, Oquab et al., 2023). A student matches an exponential-moving-average teacher across augmented views. Strong frozen features for retrieval and segmentation. Heavier training recipe, collapse must be prevented.
  • Contrastive image–text (CLIP and SigLIP, Chapter 4). Needs captions. Buys zero-shot classification and a shared space with language, which is what a VLM wants.

Masked Autoencoding from Scratch

A 224-pixel image at patch 16 is 196 patches. At a 75% mask the encoder runs on the remaining quarter. The decoder, which is smaller, sees mask tokens in the gaps and predicts pixels. At inference you throw the decoder away and keep the encoder.

N_{\mathrm{visible}} = N - \mathrm{round}(0.75\, N) = 196 - 147 = 49
keep (2, 49) masked per image [147. 147.]
Why the Mask Ratio Is an Engineering Knob
  • A low mask ratio makes pixel prediction easy (the neighbours leak the answer) and the encoder expensive (it sees almost every patch).
  • A high mask ratio forces the encoder to use context and cuts the quadratic cost of the encoder's attention, because attention runs on the visible set. The MAE paper reports that skipping masked tokens in the encoder speeds pretraining up by 3× or more Documented (He et al., 2021).
  • 75% is the paper's operating point for images, not a law. VideoMAE uses 90% or more because neighbouring frames leak even more (Chapter 10).

An honest negative result

  • The reconstruction loss falls steadily, so the MAE learned something. The frozen probe on its features is no better than a random encoder, and initialising from it makes fine-tuning worse than training from scratch.
  • This does not contradict the paper; it reproduces its caveats. None of those conditions holds on a 16×16 toy.
  • What the toy's reconstruction objective rewards is predicting flat colour from neighbours. On these images, that is almost all of the pixel variance, and shape is a small residual. A task-agnostic objective learns what dominates the loss, not what you care about.
  • Chapter 4 runs the same probe on a CLIP-trained encoder and gets 0.946. The comparison is the lesson: the objective decides what the frozen features contain.

Self-Distillation: Why DINO Needs Centring

DINO-style training compares a student's softmax output to a teacher's on two views of an image; the teacher is an EMA of the student. The obvious failure is collapse: if every image produces the same target, the student matches it trivially. DINO prevents that with two opposing operations on the teacher's logits: centring (subtract a running mean) stops one dimension dominating, and sharpening (a low temperature) stops the target becoming uniform.

no centering, temp 0.04  target entropy=0.00 (max 2.77) mean mass on dim0=1.00 distinct argmax=1
centering, temp 1.0      target entropy=2.73 (max 2.77) mean mass on dim0=0.06 distinct argmax=16
centering, temp 0.04     target entropy=0.45 (max 2.77) mean mass on dim0=0.05 distinct argmax=16
  • Sharpening alone: every image's target puts all its mass on dimension 0. Entropy 0, one distinct argmax. The student can satisfy this by ignoring the input: collapse.
  • Centring alone (temperature 1): targets are nearly uniform (entropy 2.73 of a possible 2.77). No collapse to one dimension, but almost no signal to learn from either.
  • Both: targets are peaked per image (entropy 0.45) and spread over all 16 dimensions. This is the regime DINO trains in. DINOv2 adds curated data (LVD-142M), a patch-level masked objective and scale Documented (Oquab et al., 2023).

Resolution Handling

Interpolating position embeddings

A ViT's learned position table has one row per grid cell. Feed a larger image and the grid grows; the table no longer fits. The standard move is to reshape the table into its 2-D grid, resize it with bicubic interpolation, and flatten it back. It is a heuristic, and it costs accuracy until you fine-tune at the new size.

native 16px, grid 4x4: acc=0.980
24px without resizing positions: The size of tensor a (37) must match the size of tensor b (17) at non-singleton 
24px, grid 6x6, bicubic-interpolated positions: acc=0.862

Accuracy drops by about 12 points with no retraining: the content is identical, but each patch now covers two-thirds as much of the object and sits at an interpolated position the model never saw. This is why "just send a bigger image" is not free even when the context budget allows it.

Tiling and dynamic resolution

Two production answers exist. Tiling ("any resolution") cuts a large image into encoder-sized crops plus a low-resolution thumbnail, encodes each crop at the native size, and concatenates the tokens. Dynamic resolution resizes the image to a multiple of the patch size near its native aspect ratio and lets the grid, and the token count, vary per image; it requires position encoding that works for any grid (Qwen2-VL uses 2-D rotary positions).

  • A tile of 448 px through a patch-14 encoder with 2×2 token merging is 256 tokens, so 13 tiles is about 3,300 tokens for a portrait page. The planner upscaled 1100×850 to 1792×1344: the aspect ratio matched, but pixels were invented.
  • Dynamic resolution keeps the aspect ratio and lets the cap decide: every large image lands near the 1,280-token ceiling we set (max_pixels; the Qwen2-VL model card documents these as tunable processor arguments). A 300-pixel icon costs 121 tokens instead of a full tile.
  • Neither is free. Tiling breaks objects across tile borders; dynamic resolution needs position encoding that generalises across grids. Both make token counts data-dependent, so latency budgets must use the tail of your image-size distribution.

Key Idea
  • An encoder is its weights plus its input resolution, normalisation constants, pooling and position scheme. Changing any of these makes it a different encoder. Record them in the model card of your system.
  • Choose the objective by what the frozen features must contain: language alignment (CLIP/SigLIP), dense spatial structure (DINOv2), or a fine-tuning initialisation (MAE).
  • Pooling away the spatial grid and then asking the vector "where is the total?". Mean-pooled features are for retrieval. Location questions need the patch tokens or a detector.
  • Judging MAE by a frozen linear probe and discarding it, or judging it by reconstruction loss and shipping it. The measurement that matters is your downstream task after the adaptation you will actually do.
  • Mixing two encoders' features in one index because both are "ViT-L". The spaces are not the same.
  • Normalisation mismatch. Feeding 0–255 pixels to an encoder trained on ImageNet-normalised inputs produces plausible-looking garbage features.

Contrastive Image–Text Learning

CLIP trains an image encoder and a text encoder so that matched pairs score higher than the other pairs in the batch. The loss is symmetric cross-entropy on a similarity matrix. That matrix is also a retrieval index and a zero-shot classifier, and the encoder it produces is the front end of most vision–language models.

The Engineering Problem

You want to search a photo library with a sentence, and you want a classifier for labels you did not have at training time ("hi-vis jacket", "damaged pallet"). A detector needs a box and a fixed class list. A captioning model writes prose you then have to score. You need a shared vector space in which "a photo of a dog" lands next to pictures of dogs, built from the image–text pairs the web already contains.

Possible Solutions

  • Generative captioning. Predict the next token of a caption. Good at fluent text. The gradient does not directly arrange a retrieval space, and scoring a candidate label needs a full decode.
  • Batch contrastive loss (Radford et al., 2021). In a batch of B pairs, each image should pick its text out of B texts, and each text its image. The batch is the set of negatives, so small batches are a weak version of the objective, and duplicate captions in a batch become false negatives.
  • Sigmoid contrastive loss (SigLIP, Zhai et al., 2023). Each pair is its own binary decision, positive on the diagonal and negative elsewhere. No softmax over the batch, so the batch stops being the partition function and the loss shards cleanly across devices.

The Symmetric Loss

\mathbf{L} = \frac{1}{\tau}\,\hat{\mathbf{I}}\hat{\mathbf{T}}^\top, \qquad \mathcal{L} = \tfrac{1}{2}\big(\mathrm{CE}(\mathbf{L}, \mathbf{y}) + \mathrm{CE}(\mathbf{L}^\top, \mathbf{y})\big)

\hat{\mathbf{I}} and \hat{\mathbf{T}} are L2-normalised. \mathbf{y} is the diagonal. \tau is a temperature; CLIP learns \log(1/\tau), initialised at 1/0.07 and clipped at 100 Documented (Radford et al., 2021). A small temperature makes the softmax sharp, so a slightly wrong pairing is punished hard.

Contrastive image–text

  • One space for search, classification and as the vision front end of a VLM.
  • Strong frozen features for semantics (0.946 probe versus 0.44 for MAE here).

Costs

  • The batch is the negative set; duplicates are false negatives.
  • Global vectors miss position and counting (Chapter 5 shows the consequences).
  • Cosine similarity is not a calibrated probability. Do not threshold 0.25 as "true" without a validation set.
  • Forgetting to L2-normalise before the dot product. Magnitude then dominates, and a long vector wins every retrieval.
  • Training with a temperature copied from a paper and a batch a hundred times smaller. The loss surface is not the one in the paper.
  • Narrow-domain fine-tuning with many duplicate captions ("invoice", "invoice", "invoice"). The model is penalised for being right.
  • Using a CLIP score as a faithfulness check for a caption that describes a small region. Global embeddings miss local facts. That shows up again as CLIPScore in Volume 20.

Vision–Language Models

A vision–language model lets a language model condition on image tokens. The cheap version freezes the vision encoder, trains a small connector, and fine-tunes the LM on image–question–answer data. This chapter builds exactly that on the Chapter 4 encoder, measures what each piece buys, and finds a skill the model learns last and a blind spot the language prior hides.

The Engineering Problem

You already have a language model people trust for text and a vision encoder that retrieves images. You want "what is the total on this invoice?" or "which side is the forklift on?" answered in a sentence. Fine-tuning the whole language model on every new document type is expensive and erodes text skill. Ignoring the image and hoping OCR is enough fails on stamps and layout. You need the smallest trainable bridge that works, and a way to prove the model is looking at the image rather than guessing.

Possible Solutions

  • Flamingo (Alayrac et al., 2022). A Perceiver Resampler compresses vision features to 64 tokens; gated cross-attention layers inserted into a frozen LM read them. Interleaved image and text is natural. You own new layers inside the LM.
  • BLIP-2 (Li et al., 2023). A Q-Former with 32 learned queries, trained in two stages (representation learning against the frozen image encoder, then generative learning against the frozen LM), hands a fixed small set of tokens to the LM. The bottleneck is the design, and it is cheap to train.
  • LLaVA (Liu et al., 2023). A projection (linear in v1, a two-layer MLP in v1.5) maps every patch token into the LM's embedding space and prepends them to the text. Stage 1 trains the projector on image–caption pairs with everything else frozen; stage 2 fine-tunes projector and LM on 158K visual instruction examples Documented (Liu et al., 2023).
  • Native dynamic resolution (Qwen2-VL, Wang et al., 2024). Same prefix idea, but the number of image tokens follows the image's resolution, adjacent 2×2 tokens are merged by an MLP, and positions are 2-D/3-D rotary (M-RoPE).

Simple Versus Production

Train a connector so that a frozen encoder's tokens, concatenated with a question, make a frozen or lightly tuned LM emit the answer. Instruction data is (image, question, answer) triples with the loss on the answer. That is this chapter's 2-block model.

  • Two-stage recipes: align the connector on captions with the LM frozen, then instruction-tune connector and LM (often with LoRA) on a mixture that must include OCR, charts, counting, grounding, negation, refusal and plenty of text-only data to protect text skill.
  • Dynamic resolution or tiling (Chapter 3), so the token count follows the image. Latency and memory budgets must use the tail, not the square crop you tested.
  • Tile and prefix caching: a page asked ten questions should be encoded once. The KV cache of the image prefix is reusable across questions if the prefix is byte-identical.
  • Grounding outputs (boxes or points as text tokens) so answers can be checked, which turns part of Chapter 7 into an LM output format.
  • The instruction mix decides the skills; nothing in the architecture guarantees "where", counting or reading small text. Each needs data, and each competes for training budget.
  • Unfreezing the LM trades text ability for visual ability. Both need regression tests (Volume 10).
  • Token counts become data-dependent, which breaks static batching assumptions in serving (Volume 12).

Document AI and OCR

Documents are images that mostly contain text, plus the layout that OCR throws away if you only keep the string. This chapter runs a real OCR engine on rendered pages, measures it with character and word error rates under realistic degradation, shows how reading order breaks on two columns, and retrieves pages by late interaction before anyone reads them.

The Engineering Problem

A page has a total, a handwritten note, a stamp and a table. A classical OCR engine returns a string with two zeros read as letters. An OCR-free model eats the page image and sometimes invents a row. A retrieval system has to find the right page among a million before either reads it. You need a metric for the string, a path for the layout, a retrieval index for page images, and a rule for when the image tokens are worth it.

Possible Solutions

  • Classical OCR (Tesseract, Smith, 2007, and successors). Page layout analysis finds blocks and lines; a recogniser reads each line; output is text plus word boxes and confidences. Since version 4, Tesseract's recogniser is an LSTM line model Documented (Tesseract project documentation). Fast on CPU, strong on clean print; weak on handwriting, complex layout and low resolution.
  • OCR-free document models (Donut, Kim et al., 2021; Nougat, Blecher et al., 2023). An image encoder and a text decoder emit text, JSON or markdown directly. They keep layout in the pixels. Nougat reports repetition loops as a characteristic failure Documented (Blecher et al., 2023): a fluent decoder can fail in ways a character tally does not flag.
  • General VLMs on page images. Flexible, handle stamps and charts, expensive per page (Chapter 11), and prone to confident misreads of digits.
  • Page-image retrieval (ColPali, Faysse et al., 2024). Embed each page as a set of patch vectors with a VLM backbone and score a query by late interaction, the ColBERT pattern from Volume 14. You retrieve the page before anyone OCRs it.

Production layout models detect columns, tables and reading order as a separate step, and your evaluation needs a check that operates on order, not only on characters.

Detection and Segmentation

Classification says what is in the image. Detection says where, as boxes. Segmentation says which pixels. A language model does not replace the matching problem: predicted boxes and true boxes are unordered sets. This chapter builds the geometry, matching and evaluation from scratch, checks each against torchvision and SciPy, and trains a small DETR-style set predictor on a laptop.

The Engineering Problem

A warehouse camera must count pallets and a form parser must find the signature line. A global CLIP vector can say "warehouse" and cannot say which pallet. A VLM can say "there is a signature on the left" without giving you pixels you can crop. Downstream code wants coordinates, a count that is right, and a metric that does not depend on the order in which boxes were listed.

Possible Solutions

  • One-stage grid detectors (the YOLO line, Redmon et al., 2015 and after). The image is an S\times S grid; each cell predicts boxes, objectness and classes in one forward. YOLO v1 used a 7×7 grid and ran at 45 frames per second Documented (Redmon et al., 2015). Duplicate boxes need non-maximum suppression (NMS).
  • Set prediction (DETR, Carion et al., 2020). A fixed set of learned queries attends to image features; training matches predictions to ground truth with the Hungarian algorithm so query order does not matter. No anchors, no NMS by design. The paper trained for 500 epochs on COCO, and slow convergence is its documented weakness Documented (Carion et al., 2020).
  • Promptable segmentation (SAM, Kirillov et al., 2023). A heavy image encoder runs once; a light decoder turns a prompt (point, box, mask) into masks in tens of milliseconds. Trained on SA-1B, 1.1 billion masks over 11 million images Documented (Kirillov et al., 2023). It segments; it does not name.
  • VLM grounding: the language model emits box coordinates as text. Open vocabulary, easy to integrate, and usually less precise than a detector trained for the job.

Geometry: IoU and GIoU

\mathrm{IoU}(A, B) = \frac{|A \cap B|}{|A \cup B|}, \qquad \mathrm{GIoU}(A, B) = \mathrm{IoU}(A, B) - \frac{|C \setminus (A \cup B)|}{|C|}

C is the smallest box enclosing both. IoU is zero for every pair of disjoint boxes, so as a loss it gives no gradient telling a prediction which way to move. GIoU keeps decreasing as disjoint boxes move apart.

Duplicates: Non-Maximum Suppression

nms kept ours=9 torchvision=9 identical=True

Five objects, nine survivors: noisy boxes that overlap their neighbours by less than 0.5 survive as duplicates. NMS's threshold is a counting knob. Too high and one pallet is two; too low and two adjacent pallets become one. Choose it on a validation set scored by count error, not by AP alone.

Matching Sets: Hungarian Assignment

Index alignment is the wrong comparison. The model's first box is not the annotator's first box. The minimum-cost one-to-one assignment is the right comparison, and the Hungarian algorithm finds it in O(n^3) instead of O(n!).

  • Do not ask the LM to emit raw coordinates if a detector already did it better. Use the LM where the label set is open-ended or the question needs reasoning across objects.
  • Every id the LM cites must exist; [d7] above is a hallucinated object and should fail validation, exactly like a fabricated citation in RAG (Volume 14).

Visual Reasoning and Evaluation

Once the model can see, the failure mode shifts from "it ignored the image" to "it described a plausible image that is not this one". This chapter evaluates the Chapter 5 mini-VLM the way you should evaluate a real one: balanced yes/no probes with hard negatives, inputs that contain nothing, calibrated abstention, and answer-matching rules that do not reward formatting.

The Engineering Problem

A chart question-answering demo looks excellent in a meeting. On a held-out set the model reads the title and guesses the trend the title suggests. The series goes the other way. A caption metric scores the guess as fluent. Separately, users upload a blank photo by accident, and the model describes a document that is not there. You need metrics that catch both.

What to measure, separately

  • Perception. Can the system read the text (CER), localise the object (IoU), retrieve the page (recall)? No LM judge needed.
  • Grounded answer correctness. Fields match a gold structured record: the total, the date, the highest bar. Exact or relaxed match on fields beats a paragraph score.
  • Object hallucination. Does the model claim objects that are absent? Measured with balanced yes/no probes (POPE-style) and by counting unsupported objects in captions (CHAIR-style).
  • Abstention. When the image does not contain the answer, the system says so. A benchmark that always has an answer never measures this.
  • Blind baseline. The same questions without the image. The gap is what vision contributes.

POPE-style evaluation asks "is there a X in the image?" with equal numbers of true and false objects. Accuracy alone hides a bias towards "yes", so report precision, recall and the yes-ratio. The negatives matter: random negatives are easy; near-miss negatives (same shape, wrong colour) probe whether the model binds attributes to objects.

Speech Recognition and Audio Understanding

Speech becomes a sequence of spectral frames. Recognition is sequence labelling on those frames. The two ideas you need in code are the log-mel front end and CTC; the production system around them is an encoder–decoder trained on a great deal of weakly supervised audio, wrapped in voice activity detection, chunking and diarisation. This chapter implements each piece, checks it against PyTorch and Whisper's feature extractor, and trains a CTC recogniser on synthetic speech.

The Engineering Problem

A voice note must become text you can search, with timestamps and speaker turns, in more than one language, on audio that includes silence, overlap and a door slamming. Sending the raw waveform into a Transformer at 16,000 samples a second is 16,000 tokens per second. Nobody does that. You need a compact front end, an alignment-free training objective, a decoder, and a way to handle a 40-minute meeting with a model that sees 30 seconds at a time.

Possible Solutions

  • CTC acoustic models (Graves et al., 2006). Emit a label or a blank per frame; train by summing over all alignments. Non-autoregressive, fast, streaming-friendly. Each frame's output is conditionally independent, so language knowledge must come from a decoder or an external LM.
  • Self-supervised encoders plus CTC (wav2vec 2.0, Baevski et al., 2020). Pretrain on unlabelled audio by masking latent frames and solving a contrastive task over quantised targets; fine-tune with CTC on little labelled data. The paper reports usable recognition from ten minutes of labelled speech Documented (Baevski et al., 2020).
  • Encoder–decoder ASR (Whisper, Radford et al., 2022). A Transformer encoder on 80-channel log-mel frames and an autoregressive text decoder, trained on 680,000 hours of weakly supervised multilingual audio with task tokens for transcription, translation, language id and timestamps Documented (Radford et al., 2022). Robust; slower; can hallucinate text on silence.
  • Audio classification and embeddings (AST, Gong et al., 2021). Treat the spectrogram as an image, cut overlapping patches, run a ViT. For events, music, speaker or emotion rather than words.

The Front End: Log-Mel

A short-time Fourier transform over 25 ms windows with a 10 ms hop gives 100 frames per second; a mel filterbank pools frequency bins with spacing that follows pitch perception; a log compresses the dynamic range. The result is an image with time on one axis and about 80 mel bins on the other.

logmel (7, 40) peak_mel_bin=7 centre_hz=438

A 440 Hz sine (the first 1,600 samples at 16 kHz, 512-point window, hop 160) yields 7 frames and 40 mel bins; the peak bin's filter is centred at 438 Hz. The front end found the tone.

What the library abstracts, and why it matters

  • Both find 440 Hz, in different bins (our bin 15, Whisper's bin 11). Whisper's filterbank uses the Slaney mel scale with area normalisation; ours uses the HTK formula. Same physics, different coordinates. A checkpoint trained on one set of coordinates fed the other gets shifted inputs and degrades silently. The front end is part of the checkpoint.
  • Every input becomes 3,000 frames: Whisper pads or trims to 30 seconds. Our 5-second clip used 502 frames; 83% of the encoder's work on this request is padding. Whisper's encoder then downsamples by 2 with a strided convolution to 1,500 positions Documented (Radford et al., 2022).
  • The extractor also normalises (log10 power, clamp to 8 log10 units, i.e. 80 dB, below the maximum, then rescale). Those constants are part of the contract too.

CTC from Scratch

CTC extends the label sequence with blanks, \ell' = (b, \ell_1, b, \ell_2, \ldots, b), and sums the probability of every frame-level path that collapses to \ell (merge repeats, then drop blanks). The forward variable \alpha_t(s) is the total probability of prefixes ending at position s of \ell' after t frames:

\alpha_t(s) = \big(\alpha_{t-1}(s) + \alpha_{t-1}(s-1) + [\ell'_s \ne b \wedge \ell'_s \ne \ell'_{s-2}]\,\alpha_{t-1}(s-2)\big)\; y_t(\ell'_s)
[1, 2, 3] 11.711597 11.711597
[3, 3] 13.820511 13.820511
[1, 2, 2, 4] 13.236145 13.236145

Identical to six decimals, including the repeated-label cases where the skip transition is forbidden. The repeat rule is the whole trick: between two identical labels a blank is compulsory, which is also why greedy decoding keeps "ll" in "hello".

Decoding: greedy and prefix beam search

ctc [1, 2, 3, 3, 4]
greedy: [] beam: [((1,), 0.64), ((), 0.36)]

The argmax path is blank–blank (0.36), so greedy outputs nothing. But three paths collapse to "a" (a–a, a–blank, blank–a) with total probability 0.64. Prefix beam search tracks, per prefix, the probability of ending in a blank and of ending in its last label, and so sums over paths. It is also where an external language model or lexicon is fused.

Video Understanding

Video is a stack of images with a budget you cannot pay. The first design choice is which frames enter the model. The second is whether time is a separate axis of attention, a dimension of the patch, or just a longer sequence. This chapter measures what sampling misses, retrieves a moment from frame embeddings, and prices the attention schemes.

The Engineering Problem

A ten-minute clip at 30 frames a second is 18,000 frames. At 196 tokens a frame that is 3.5 million tokens. The question "did anyone enter after the alarm?" depends on a few seconds. Uniform sampling might hit them. It might hit the ceiling. The team needs a sampling policy with a stated temporal resolution, a way to find the relevant moment cheaply, and a model whose cost does not grow with the square of the clip length.

Possible Solutions

  • Uniform or sparse sampling. A fixed count of frames spread over the clip, each encoded as an image. Cheap and blind to events between the samples.
  • Content-aware sampling. Keep frames where the picture changes (shot boundaries, motion). Cheap to compute, good for surveillance-like static cameras; fooled by camera motion and lighting changes.
  • Divided space–time attention (TimeSformer, Bertasius et al., 2021). Each patch attends across time at its own location, then across space within its frame. The paper found divided attention the best of the variants it compared Documented (Bertasius et al., 2021).
  • Tubelets (ViViT, Arnab et al., 2021). The patch extends in time: a t \times P \times P cube becomes one token, cutting tokens by t. Motion inside the cube is inside the token.
  • Masked video pretraining (VideoMAE, Tong et al., 2022). Tube masking at 90–95% ratios, because neighbouring frames leak Documented (Tong et al., 2022). Same idea as Chapter 3, stricter redundancy.
  • Retrieve, then read. Embed frames cheaply, find candidate moments by text query, and send only those to an expensive VLM.

  • Change-based sampling puts five of eight frames inside the event (140–150), plus the frame where it ends (152) and one noise frame (240). On a static camera this is nearly free; with a moving camera every frame changes and the heuristic degrades to noise.
  • The existing sample_frame_indices in structs.py includes the last frame on purpose; a naive range(0, n, stride) often drops it, and with it the end of the clip.

Production Multimodal Systems

Production is preprocessing, a token budget, a latency budget, and a privacy rule for media you would not put in a text log. The model choice is downstream of those four. This chapter turns vendor token formulas into code, prices a document workload, and builds the gates that run before any pixel reaches a model.

The Engineering Problem

Users upload phone photos of documents, 20-minute voice notes and the occasional video. The bill is dominated by image tokens and by audio longer than the model's chunk. A 12-megapixel photo arrives sideways with GPS coordinates in its metadata. The same screenshot is uploaded forty times. A log pipeline stores the raw uploads next to text prompts. That is a new class of data you now retain.

Decisions that belong in the design review

  • Normalise before you embed. EXIF orientation, colour space, sample rate, mono versus stereo. Two "identical" photos that differ by a rotation are different token grids.
  • Cap the budget. Maximum side length, pixels, images per request, audio seconds, frames. Reject or downsample past the cap. Do not discover the cap as an out-of-memory error on the GPU.
  • Price the representation. OCR text, a downscaled image, tiles or full resolution: each has a token count you can compute before calling a model.
  • Store derivatives on purpose. Keep OCR text and boxes if you will ask many questions. Keep the image if you will ask about stamps. Delete the upload you do not need, and write down the retention.
  • Separate the untrusted channel. Pixels and audio can contain instructions. Tool calls that follow a media question still pass the harness from Volume 16.

Review and Next Steps

What You Learned

  • Multimodal systems differ in where modalities meet: pipelines of specialists, early fusion, late fusion (dual encoders), projection (prefix image tokens) or cross-attention. Prefix fusion costs L(T+I)^2 attention pairs; cross-attention costs LT^2 + (L/k)TI (Chapter 1).
  • Image tokens scale with (H/P)^2. A 1024-pixel page is 4,096 tokens at patch 16 and 1,024 at patch 32. Gated cross-attention leaves a frozen LM unchanged at initialisation (Chapter 1).
  • A ViT is patchify, a linear map (a strided convolution in practice), positions and Transformer blocks. It trails a CNN at 500 images (0.787 versus 0.993) and nearly catches it at 5,000 (Chapter 2).
  • The pretraining objective decides what frozen features contain: MAE features did not help at toy scale, CLIP features probe at 0.946. DINO needs centring and sharpening to avoid collapse. Interpolated positions cost accuracy (0.98 → 0.86); tiling and dynamic resolution make token counts data-dependent (Chapter 3).
  • CLIP's loss is symmetric cross-entropy over a batch similarity matrix; duplicate captions impose a loss floor (1.63 at batch 64 over 14 captions). A dual encoder composed unseen colour–shape pairs half the time. Zero-shot classification is retrieval (Chapter 4).
  • A mini-VLM with a frozen encoder learned colour and shape quickly and "side" only with more training, although a probe showed the patch tokens carried it (0.977). A text-only baseline is mandatory (Chapter 5).
  • Tesseract reads a clean invoice perfectly; at 0.35 scale CER is 0.73 and the total is lost. Whitespace rules alone moved CER from 0.245 to 0.000. Naive reading order interleaves columns. MaxSim late interaction beat one vector per page (0.95 versus 0.73 recall@1) (Chapter 6).
  • IoU, GIoU and NMS match torchvision exactly; the Hungarian algorithm matches brute force in microseconds. A tiny DETR reached mAP@0.5 of 0.99 on synthetic scenes and still fired 5% extra queries (Chapter 7).
  • POPE-style probes need hard negatives; late fusion lost precision on near-miss negatives. A VLM answered "blue" with 0.94 confidence for 196 of 200 blank images; confidence thresholds trade coverage for accuracy (Chapter 8).
  • Log-mel conventions differ between libraries (bin 15 versus bin 11 for the same 440 Hz tone). CTC from scratch matches PyTorch to six decimals. A CTC recogniser trained on clean audio hit WER 0.76 on noisy audio; noise augmentation fixed it (Chapter 9).
  • Uniform sampling of 8 frames missed a 0.4 s event; change-based sampling and moment retrieval found it. Divided attention scores 57× fewer pairs than joint attention at 80 frames (Chapter 10).
  • Vendor image-token formulas give 765–1,600 tokens for a page whose OCR text is 122 tokens. Gates, EXIF normalisation, perceptual-hash dedup and pixel redaction run before the model (Chapter 11).

Important Architecture

Failure Modes

  • Quoting a vision model without its token count at the resolution you will serve; planning from a token formula never reconciled with a bill.
  • A class token or a mean pool used to answer "where".
  • Changing input resolution, normalisation or mel conventions without treating it as a new encoder.
  • Contrastive loss at a paper's temperature and a toy batch, then treated as the paper's result; duplicate captions as negatives.
  • A connector that is cheap in parameters and expensive in context; a headline VQA accuracy hiding a skill at chance.
  • CER without whitespace rules, averaged over easy pages; characters evaluated and reading order never.
  • IoU computed on unsorted lists; NMS thresholds tuned for AP instead of count error.
  • No blind baseline, no empty inputs, no abstain answer; a visual judge that never saw the image.
  • An audio front end that does not match the checkpoint; CTC decoded without blanks; clean-only training data.
  • Eight frames called a video; the last frame dropped; events shorter than the sampling gap.
  • Raw uploads, EXIF and base64 media retained in traces and caches.

  1. Ingest. Normalise orientation, strip metadata, dedup by dHash, gate sizes. Render or scan at least 50 pages including invoices, a two-column policy and five bar charts drawn from known tables.
  2. Read. OCR every page with word boxes. Store text, boxes, confidences and a gold transcription of the total, date and one table per page on 30 pages; report field-level CER and a reading-order check.
  3. Retrieve. Embed page crops with a frozen encoder you can run locally; compare one vector per page with MaxSim over crops on 30 queries (recall@1, recall@5).
  4. Answer. Answer from OCR text when field confidence is inside your cap. Fall back to an image prompt (a local VLM, or a hosted one if you choose) only when the question is about a mark OCR dropped or confidence is low; log the token count of the fallback using the Chapter 11 estimators.
  5. Evaluate. A golden set of at least 60 questions: printed fields (exact match), chart values (relaxed 5% match), five absent-field questions and five blank or unreadable pages (abstention), and five pages with an instruction aimed at a tool (the Volume 16 policy must hold). Report a blind baseline.
  6. Cost. Report tokens and dollars per page for the OCR path and the image path at your chosen prices, p95 latency for each, and the fraction of questions routed to the fallback.

Unit tests run offline on rendered pages; the hosted-model path is an integration test behind the same interface.

Exercises

  1. Add a two-layer MLP to TinyViT and show the parameter count still matches the formula.
  2. Plot ViT and CNN test accuracy against training-set size (250 to 5,000). Where do the curves cross, if at all?
  3. Retrain TinyMAE with normalised-pixel targets and a 90% mask; report probe and fine-tune accuracy.
  4. Mask false negatives (identical captions) in the CLIP loss and rerun Chapter 4's table.
  5. Add counting questions to the mini-VLM and find the smallest resampler k that counts to three reliably.
  6. Add a "PAID" stamp to the invoice and measure what Tesseract does with it and what it does to CER elsewhere.
  7. Measure TinyDETR's count error with and without NMS at three thresholds.
  8. Add a "none" answer and blank images to the mini-VLM's training and rerun Chapter 8's coverage–accuracy table.
  9. Change the mel hop and the mel scale in log_mel and feed the features to the trained toy recogniser. Report the WER damage.
  10. Pan the synthetic camera and compare pixel-difference and embedding-difference keyframe sampling.
  11. Reconcile one of Chapter 11's estimators against real billed usage for ten images of different sizes.

Interview Questions

  1. A 1024-pixel page at patch 16 costs how many tokens, and what happens to that count if you double the patch? What detail do you expect to lose?
  2. Compare prefix fusion and cross-attention fusion in attention cost and KV-cache growth. Why is prefix fusion still the common open-model design?
  3. Why does a ViT need position embeddings when a CNN does not? What did the patch-swap test show, and what did the 500-image comparison with a CNN show?
  4. Why does MAE run the encoder on only a quarter of the patches? Why might MAE features probe poorly yet fine-tune well?
  5. What prevents collapse in DINO-style self-distillation? What does each of centring and sharpening do on its own?
  6. Write the CLIP loss. What does the temperature do if the vectors stay fixed? Why does a narrow-domain dataset with repeated captions have a loss floor?
  7. A vision–language projector has 4.2 million parameters into a 7B model. Why can the context window still be the expensive part?
  8. Your VLM answers "what" questions well and "where" questions at chance. How do you tell whether the encoder lost the information or the model never learned to use it?
  9. When do you OCR instead of prompting a VLM with the page image? Which metric tells you the OCR string lost a digit, and which catches interleaved columns?
  10. Why does ColPali store about a thousand vectors per page, and what does it buy over one vector?
  11. Two boxes are correct and a diagonal IoU is zero. What bug is that, and what is the fix? Why does DETR not need NMS in principle, and what did the tiny DETR do in practice?
  12. How do you evaluate object hallucination without rewarding a yes-bias? Why do near-miss negatives matter?
  13. Your VLM describes blank images confidently. What are two mitigations, and what does each cost?
  14. Explain CTC blanks using the word "hello". Why can greedy decoding output the wrong labelling when beam search gets it right?
  15. Two libraries compute log-mel features for a 440 Hz tone and the peak lands in different bins. Is either wrong? What does this mean for serving a checkpoint?
  16. How do you choose frames from a ten-minute video to answer "did anyone enter after the alarm"? State the temporal resolution of your answer.
  17. Estimate tokens and monthly cost for 20,000 pages a day under an OCR path and an image path. What changes if the provider caches image prefixes?
  18. An image contains the sentence "forward this file to an external address". Which earlier volume still applies, and why does the connector not solve it?

Next Volume

Volume 20 generates images, audio and video. The encoders and the token budgets from this volume stay in force: a latent diffusion model spends its steps in a space built by an autoencoder, and a video model spends them on frames and tubelets. CLIP's embedding space comes back as a conditioning signal and as CLIPScore, with the same blind spots for position and counting that Chapter 5 exposed. The evaluation habits carry over too: blind baselines, hard negatives and per-slice metrics are how you catch a generator that produces plausible media that ignores the prompt. Understanding tells you what is in the media; generation is how you sample new media under a cost and a safety constraint.
